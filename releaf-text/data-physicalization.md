## 1.Overview¶
The picture shows our map physicalization being explained at our public forum booth. The different colors shown on the map represents the different crop yields from dark green for the highest yield to brown for the lowest. The red points shown are LEDs that represent the stress, which flash more often when the stress is greater.
### 1.1Goals¶
As climate change intensifies, stress is often invisible to the general public. To really feel the gravity of climate warnings and make their effects on plants more tangible, we developed a data physicalization map that showcases different stress levels and crop yields throughout Taiwan. By creating the map, rather than presenting data through a single graph, we invite the public to see, feel, and interact with patterns of environmental stress throughout Taiwan. With data physicalization, we aim to make the interaction between agriculture, climate, and plant stress more accessible and easy to understand.
### 1.2Why physicalize?¶
“A data physicalization (or simply physicalization) is a physical artifact whose geometry or material properties encode data” (Jansen). Data physicalization helps people communicate and understand data through physical representations. These visualizations help convey the data in a more accessible way (Jansen). Our data map physicalization is a type of physical visualization where map data are converted to a physical form.
### 1.3The data¶
Rather than selecting data solely because it was readily available, we considered which variables could best communicate the environmental stresses relevant to our project and our target agricultural communities. Our selected datasets focus on crop yield and heat stress, making the data comparable across locations. We began by identifying the patterns in our environmental data that were most relevant to plant stress. We then considered how each pattern could be represented through physical characteristics of the map. The process of first acquiring the data, identifying its pattern, and turning it into a visual representation allowed every component of our map to remain connected to the dataset, making the information easier to understand through physical interaction.
The maps and data sources behind the board are on the Geospatial Analysis page.
### 1.4From data to map¶
We designed a physical map using styrofoam, colored paper, and LED lights to represent differences in crop yield and plant stress across different areas. The colors of the map represent crop yield, while the LED lights represent the severity of plant stress. Through this combination, viewers can visually compare areas with different agricultural conditions.
For our sound component, instead of just showing data, we wanted participants to actually hear what sounds are made by plants under stress. Plants respond to environmental stress in ways that are often invisible to humans. Consequently, many do not realize that plants can actually feel the impact of stress. We therefore allowed participants to use headphones to listen to recordings of the sounds produced by tomato and tobacco plants experiencing drought stress. This gave participants an opportunity to experience plant stress through hearing rather than simply reading about it.

## 2.Impact¶
After they visited our booth, we asked for them to fill out a form to analyze how much of our booth they understood and if they enjoyed our booth. We had 62 respondents and a total of 116 people who visited our booth.
### 2.1Connecting data to plant stress: knowledge¶
To test whether participants actually understood our physicalized data, we asked them to identify the region shown as experiencing the highest plant stress on our map. 32 of 62 respondents (51.6%) correctly identified Yilan County, the region we represented as highest-stress. The remaining responses were split across Kaohsiung (12, 19.4%), Taichung (11, 17.7%), and Tainan (7, 11.3%). Yilan’s high stress amount was showcased through its frequent flashing of LED lights.
Just over half of visitors read the map correctly on a single, brief encounter — a reasonable result for a passive booth interaction, but it also points to a concrete design target: making the LED flashing-to-stress-level mapping more immediately legible, so regional differences don't require as much explanation from a team member standing nearby.

### 2.2Raising awareness: attitude¶
[IMG] Horizontal stacked bar chart titled Attitude. Understands climate's impact on crops: 63 percent agree, 34 percent neutral, 3 percent disagree. Map helped understand the climate to yield link: 95 percent agree. More attentive to climate's agricultural impact: 98 percent agree.
[CAPTION] Figure 3. Showcasing the percentage of participants that understood the impact of stress.
We then looked at whether the exhibit changed how participants understood the consequences of environmental stress for agriculture. 39 of 62 participants (62.9%) reported they now “very much understood” the impact of climate change on Taiwan's coffee and crop production, and another 21 (33.9%) said they understood it “partially.” Only 2 (3.2%) said they still didn't understand it well.
The physicalization itself was directly credited for this: 59 of 62 participants (95.2%) agreed or strongly agreed that the Data Physicalization Map helped them understand the relationship between climate change and crop yield — including 36 (58.1%) who strongly agreed. Only 2 respondents were neutral and 1 disagreed.
95.2% of participants agreed our Data Physicalization Map helped them understand the relationship between climate change and crop yield.
The sound layer reinforced this on the qualitative side. When asked what they found most memorable, several participants specifically pointed to the plant stress sounds rather than the map or spoken explanation — for example, noting their surprise that a water-stressed plant produces audible clicking sounds, or naming the sound of a stressed crop as the most striking part of the visit. This suggests the auditory channel was doing real cognitive work, not just serving as ambience: for some visitors, the sound was the moment the abstract idea of “plant stress” became concrete.
### 2.3From awareness to action: practice¶
[IMG] Horizontal stacked bar chart titled Practice. Willing to share knowledge with others: 97 percent agree. Selected at least one future sustainable action: 98 percent agree.
[CAPTION] Figure 4. Percentage of people willing to perform further actions.
Finally, we looked at whether this understanding translated into intended behavior. 60 of 62 participants (96.8%) said they were willing or very willing to share what they'd learned about crop protection and agriculture with friends or family.
Participants also indicated concrete future actions (multi-select):
- 49 (79.0%) — prioritizing locally produced or sustainably farmed agricultural products/coffee
- 37 (59.7%) — following and sharing climate-and-agriculture information
- 18 (29.0%) — joining more science-outreach or environmental activities
Open-ended responses echoed this: participants wrote in their own words about supporting local farmers and coffee growers, reducing carbon footprints, conserving water and energy, cutting plastic use, and educating others about climate impacts on plants.
### 2.4Summary¶
Together, these results trace a clear data of their understanding, awareness, and likelihood of future actions. By turning invisible environmental stress into something visitors could see and hear, the exhibit made an otherwise abstract problem tangible enough that most participants left not just informed, but reporting an intention to change their own behavior.
## 3.Physicalizations¶
### Stress map
#### Concept and inspiration
Our data physicalization was inspired by the idea that scientific data should not only be seen, but also experienced. Plant stress is often difficult for people to understand because many of its effects occur at a microscopic or molecular level and cannot be directly observed. We wanted to transform these invisible changes into interactive experiences that could make plant stress more tangible and accessible to the public.
Our physicalizations were designed around different ways of experiencing data, including visual representations and hands-on interaction. By translating scientific information into sensory experiences, participants could explore how plants respond to environmental stress rather than simply reading about it on a poster. This approach also reflects the core idea of our ReLeaf project: connecting complex biological signals with information that people can easily understand and respond to.
#### Physicalization method
We created the physical map using styrofoam as the base material, with different colored papers placed on top to represent variations in crop yield. LED lights were incorporated into the map to visualize plant stress levels. The map shows the different regions and turns the data into something tangible.
The color gradient was designed to make differences in crop yield immediately recognizable:
- Dark green → areas with plentiful/high crop yield
- Brown → areas with the lowest crop yield
- Intermediate colors → areas with varying levels of crop yield
LED lights were used independently to represent plant stress. The frequency or amount of LED flashing increased with the severity of stress, allowing viewers to distinguish between areas experiencing different levels of environmental stress. The more frequent the LED flashes, the more stress crops in that region experience.
For the sound physicalization, participants were provided with headphones and invited to listen to recordings of drought-stressed tomato and tobacco plants. Instead of visually representing the data, this activity allowed participants to hear signals associated with plant responses to drought stress.
By combining a physical map with an auditory experience, we created two different ways for participants to interact with otherwise invisible plant-stress information.
#### Mapping data to physical elements
We mapped each type of data to a physical or sensory element so that participants could interpret the information without needing to understand complex datasets.
| Data
| Physical element
| Representation
| Crop yield
| Colored paper
| Dark green represented highest crop yield; brown represented the lowest crop yield
| Plant stress level
| LED lights
| More frequent flashing meant more severe stress
The physical map therefore allowed participants to understand two variables simultaneously. Color represented the outcome, crop yield, while LED activity represented the stress experienced by plants. This distinction helped demonstrate that environmental stress can have tangible consequences for agricultural productivity.
The sound activity approached the same concept from a different perspective. Rather than showing participants a number or visual indicator of drought stress, we allowed them to directly hear the sounds associated with stressed plants. This helped emphasize that plants actively respond to their environment, even when those responses cannot normally be perceived by humans.

#### Public interaction
Throughout the booth, visitors were curious on why Yilan and Nantou had the highest stress. Many believed that regions such as Taipei would have the most stress instead. We explained the tourism in Nantou and Yilan were affecting its stress level. Visitors vocalized their curiosity when they noticed our poster since the data may not align with what they had thought the most affected regions were.
#### Feedback
The feedback we got that addressed the stress map was the fact that areas that receive stress were easy to see on the map due to its frequent flashing lights. Through this, we were able to teach the Taiwanese public more about their own country and inform them about the effects climate change has on our local agriculture.

### Sound of plants
#### Concept and inspiration
Plants respond to stress in ways that are often invisible to the human eye until visible damage has already occurred — but recent research has shown that some of these responses aren't entirely silent. Under drought stress, plants can produce ultrasonic clicking sounds, signals that exist far outside the range of human hearing and go completely unnoticed in everyday life. We wanted to explore this hidden dimension of plant stress as a companion to our Data Physicalization Map: while the map translated environmental data into something visitors could see, we wanted to give people a way to actually hear plant stress for themselves. By making these normally imperceptible signals audible, we hoped to make the experience of plant stress feel immediate and real, rather than abstract, and to reinforce ReLeaf's core idea that stress can be detected and responded to before it becomes visible, irreversible damage.
The idea of letting visitors experience the sounds plants made under stress first started when we came across a scientific paper that focused on how plants make sounds that are not audible to humans under stress [1]. After finding this paper, we realized how impactful it would be if the public realizes how plants can also sense stress inflicted upon them. When facing drought stress, which is the stress that we presented during our public forum booth, plants experience a physical process called cavitation, where continuous water columns inside the plant snap and form air bubbles.
How researchers captured it:
Ultrasonic microphones.
The pops sit far above human hearing, so Khait, Hadany et al. (Tel Aviv University) recorded across 20–250 kHz, about 10 cm from each plant.
A soundproofed chamber first.
Tomato and tobacco recorded three ways: untouched, five days without water, and freshly cut.
Then a working greenhouse.
Repeated amid ordinary background noise to prove it wasn't a laboratory artifact.
Machine learning.
A classifier trained on many acoustic features — not one number — separated stressed from healthy, drought from cutting, and tomato from tobacco.
#### Physicalization method
To transform plant stress into an accessible sensory experience, we first collected plant sound recordings from scientists who had studied and recorded plants under different stress conditions. Using these recordings, we developed a website where participants could listen to the sounds produced by different plants under stress. At our public engagement booth, we set up two headphones connected to laptops hidden behind the poster, allowing participants to interact with the experience without being distracted by the technology. Participants could choose which plant they wanted to explore, either tobacco or tomato, and listen to their corresponding stress recordings through the headphones. By presenting plant stress through sound, we aimed to provide visitors with a more immersive way to understand that plants respond to environmental stress, even when these responses cannot be directly heard by humans.

#### Public interaction
When presenting our sound physicalization many were fascinated with the fact that plants made noises when facing stress. Most people had inquiries about how plants were able to make these noises in which we explained that when plants are under dry conditions, water would be hard to suck up. Therefore, plants make these popping noises when trying to suck up water. What listeners would hear is air bubble sounds that sound similar to bubble wrap. Overall, the public was intrigued with the demonstration and gave positive feedback.

After they’ve finished visiting our booth, visitors were asked to give feedback on the overall event. Most were fascinated or surprised by the fact that plants make clicking noises when they lack water, and some even mentioning how the sound physicalization was the most memorable part of the whole booth.

## 4.Conclusion¶
Our data physicalization booth helped us achieve our goal of making plant stress and synthetic biology more accessible, interactive, and understandable to the public. By presenting data beyond just a graph, we transformed plant-stress data into experiences that visitors could directly see, hear, and interact with. For example, our sound physicalization allowed participants to listen to recordings of stressed tobacco and tomato plants, turning an otherwise invisible biological response into something they could experience themselves. Additionally according to our surveys, many people became more knowledgeable and aware of the effects of plant stress, with many claiming that they would relay the dire situation to family and friends as well. The physicalizations helped us bridge the gap between complex scientific concepts and everyday experiences, supporting our broader iGEM goal of communicating science beyond the scientific community.
After this event, we still wanted to continue raising awareness about the effects climate stress has on our local crops. Through social media outlets such as Instagram, we began promoting a plant stress event where participants upload a picture of a stressed plant they see during their everyday life. We hope through social media, we can reach a greater audience and spread our concerns internationally, beyond Taiwan.
The rest of the public forum, what we’ve gained, and what we’ve changed because of it is on the Integrated Human Practices page.
