## 1.Overview¶
ReLeaf is an optogenetic bioreactor system that enables automated, on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic crop stresses through cell-free delivery of protective proteins. Our mission is to strengthen agricultural resilience by reducing crop losses while decreasing dependence on chemical interventions.
### What we plan to do
Deploy a sealed bioreactor system at the farm level that continuously monitors soil and environmental conditions, detects stress thresholds (heat, salinity, pathogens), and automatically produces and releases protective proteins directly to the crop root zone. The system operates with minimal farmer intervention and integrates seamlessly into existing irrigation systems.
### What the product does
The bioreactor houses engineered Bacillus subtilis that synthesizes three complementary protective proteins: LEA 14 (abiotic stress protection), ACCD (ethylene reduction), and NaD1 (antifungal defense), triggered by green/red light signals when stress is detected. A hollow-fiber membrane ensures complete biological containment: bacteria stay inside, only protective metabolites exit into soil.
Mission statement: ReLeaf strengthens agricultural resilience by enabling automated, on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic stresses through an optogenetic bioreactor, shifting from reactive chemical management to proactive, precision crop defense while reducing environmental contamination.
Vision: Every farmer is a biomanufacturer.
Our vision is to empower every farmer to have their small bacteria factory for producing biostimulants that help plants withstand environmental stresses. Through our bioreactor system, we wish to cut down the long bioprocessing times and the difficulties, so that farmers can benefit from science at a faster rate than the present.
[FOLD] Climate stresses & vulnerability of agriculture
In a world of climate change, where seasons shift dramatically, temperatures fluctuate uncontrollably, soil salinity escalates, and fungal outbreaks intensify, humanity is forced to choose between immediate technological adaptation or multi-system crop failure in modern agriculture. Abiotic stresses, primarily heat and soil salinity, already cause an estimated 50% of worldwide crop yield losses annually, and climate change further exacerbates the severity and frequency of these issues (Bray et al., 2000; Mittler, 2002). For example, a 1 degree Celsius rise in average growing season temperatures reduces rice yield by 6.2%. Additionally, over 20% of the world’s irrigated farmland is already significantly affected by salinity, with an additional 3 hectares becoming unproductive every minute (Zhao et al., 2017). Small farmers produce approximately 70-80% of the global food supply, a proportion that rises to over 80% of all consumed food in many developing sub-regions (Ricciardi et al., 2018). The focus on small farmers is critical because they represent the backbone of local food sovereignty. In East Asia, including Taiwan, where over 90% of agricultural holdings are small-scale family operations averaging less than 1 hectare, these small farmers directly supply more than 80% of domestic fresh vegetables and fruits (Ministry of Agriculture, 2024). For all the smallholder farmers that make up over 500 million smallholder farms, these staggering numbers mean that a single stress event can potentially wipe out an entire season’s income with no recovery mechanism or backup plan.
One of the key issues that make this crisis particularly acute is the combination of heat and salinity stress. Under field conditions, these stresses occur repeatedly, compounding cellular damage in ways that can’t be triggered by either stress independently. At the same time, plants that are weakened by stress can become vulnerable to pathogens, meaning that even a miniscule instance of salinity or heat can initiate a disease outbreak. Current agricultural systems are not equipped to handle such crises - either current solutions damage soil health and burden farmers with costs they can’t afford, or these solutions are reactive and too slow, only able to be applied after visible damage has occurred. Chemical solutions such as fungicides (ex. mancozeb, azoxystrobin, or copper based sprays) and synthetic fertilizers remain the most accessible and affordable remedies, but they only further destroy the conditions of the already stressed plants because they are only broadly applied after visible and often irreversible crop damage has already occurred.

### 2.2Product usage and function¶
ReLeaf is an integrated system that produces crop stress protectants when and where farmers need them. The system has three core components that work together to monitor field conditions, manufacture protectants on demand, and guide farmers through every step.
[IMG] A labelled diagram of the minimum viable product. A pink B. subtilis pathway runs from a magnetically stirred culture bottle with an air filter, through a pump, a flow meter and an inline photometer, up into a hollow fiber membrane with pressure transducers at both ends and samplers, and back to the bottle. A blue protectant pathway leaves the side of the hollow fiber membrane through a Luer valve and a pressure transducer.
[CAPTION] Fig. 1. Minimum viable product, the most simple sellable version of our bioreactor
The Sensors continuously monitor the farmer’s field. Leaf humidity sensors track moisture stress on leaves in real time, while our AIoT system (paired with a digital twin, a virtual model of the farm’s specific conditions) processes weather data and field conditions to forecast when crops will need protection. The digital twin learns the farmer’s patterns over time and improves its predictions so that the system gets smarter the longer it is used by the farmer. This real-time data feeds directly into the bioreactor, so production can start before stress arrives.
The Bioreactor produces crop stress protectants for the farmer. Engineered Bacillus subtilis grows in a sealed loop and secretes protectants through a hollow fiber membrane, which holds the cells back. The output is a cell-free liquid that the farmer can then use to spray on their crops. The Bacillus subtilis arrives as a sealed cartridge, so running the bioreactor itself does not require the farmers to be in a lab. The app then alerts the farmer of when to refill growth medium and when to substitute the B. subtilis. Maintenance of the bioreactor is considerably minimal since sensors and the bioreactor itself can function indoors when supplied with the prepared medium or B. subtilis cartridges, or farmers can prepare their own following our protocol.
The App brings everything together on the farmer’s phone. It tailors recommendations to each farmer’s input on the type and quantity of crops, their location, and stress conditions. Farmers will be notified of the protectant production activity, AI-forecasted stress levels, the external environment data, and bioreactor repairment. When the protectant is ready, the app will notify farmers with the exact dilution ratio for their crop type and stress level. When the bioreactor needs repair, the app gives the farmers a convenient option where they can order their new components, like the medium and the bacteria powder, in one-click. Real-time updates, historical records, and quick notifications will help farmers make better decisions about their crops based on what’s actually happening in the field itself as well as in the bioreactor.

## 3.Business model¶
### 3.1What is a social enterprise business model?¶
A social enterprise, at its core, is a business model that aims to support or assist certain demographics without sacrificing profit entirely, which makes it distinct from an Non-Governmental Organization (NGO). A good example of this is USE-GH, or Ubuntu Social Enterprise-Ghana, which is actually one of the organizations that inspired the very first project pitch for this. What they do is instead of making farmers have to go through middlemen in order to sell their product and make less money, USE-GH does it for them at a much lower rate and teaches them how to process their crops and raw materials to make it into completed products so that they can make more money. As can be seen, the aim is to be able to generate enough profit to continue running all while helping the community the social enterprise wants to support, which in our case is small farmers all over the world.
### 3.2Why a social enterprise?¶
The team is aware that not a lot of money can be made out of it, but our vision is to have a social impact, and to empower farmers, which is why we chose this business model instead of a traditional one. We want to be able to preserve our vision in the best way possible while not becoming an NGO, as then operations would be difficult due to high costs.
[IMG] A two-sided flow diagram. On the blue profit-making side, stakeholder support from Academia Sinica, professors and funds flows into GEMS Taiwan, which builds the smart engineered microbial system and sells it to large agricultural businesses, the payers. On the yellow non-profit side, funding from government and NGOs and income from the payers reaches small-scale farmers, the beneficiaries, who are then able to buy the product. The stated goal is helping small farmers increase yield and profit.

In order to better understand where our advantages and weaknesses lie, we did a SWOT analysis, carefully considering our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

Weaknesses: bioreactor reliability risks, not fully organic, cartridge maintenance costs, limited responsiveness. Opportunities: rapid market growth, climate-driven demand, regulatory tailwinds, institutional support. Threats: regulatory ambiguity, adoption resistance, cost-dependent uptake, organic certification limits.
[CAPTION] Fig. 3. Above is a graphic showing our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats with simpler bullet points
#### Strengths
The strength of our bioreactor system lies in its modular multi-chamber design, which allows for precise light-triggered release of protectants. Its cell-free delivery and hollow fiber membrane also provides safety measures that reduce safety and regulatory concerns, while electronic sensors and AI forecasting allows for accurate prediction of upcoming weather to anticipate the amount of protectants needed to both soil-based and hydroponics systems. Additionally, this on-site delivery of protectants cuts down on delivery and storage costs that would usually be associated with other protectants, allowing the bioreactor to provide a more sustainable solution to conventional methods.
#### Weaknesses
The product also has several potential weaknesses. Imperfections in the bioreactor could lead to dilution of the protectants, while biofouling inside the chambers may reduce protein yield and shorten the system’s lifespan. Malfunctions in parts such as the hollow fiber or valve could mean the blockage of protectant delivery. The product may also face limitations in countries with strict organic certification requirements because the bioreactor is not completely organic. Additionally, the product’s price may be too expensive, which can dissuade potential buyers.
#### Opportunities
The project is in a beneficial position because of the expanding biocontrol market, increasing climate-related crop stress, and increasing support for sustainable and environmentally friendly agriculture. In relation to this, many countries are also trying to phase out chemical protectants and fertilizers in favour of more sustainable alternatives, creating the perfect scenario for our bioreactor to enter.
#### Threats
Finally, several external threats could affect the success of the product. Unclear regulations surrounding cell-free agricultural biologics may delay approval, especially if the product falls between the classifications of a biological pesticide and a synthetic biology-derived compound. Farmer adoption may also be limited by unfamiliarity with the technology, concerns about cost-effectiveness, and reliance on traditional chemical pesticides. In addition, some farmers may view synthetic biology as too risky, while potential incompatibility with organic farmers can cause loss of clientele.

Social factors are critical to the success of our product. The growing consumer demand for eco-friendly farming practices aligns well with our value proposition, although we must navigate cultural attitudes toward agricultural innovation in different markets.
Technological advancement is also a key component. Standardized manufacturing processes for hardware and sensors are able to reduce scaling barriers.
Environmental pressures also pose benefits and risks. Severe climate threats including droughts, heat waves, and fungal disease create an immediate demand for crop resilience solutions. ### Value proposition
Our unique value proposition highlights the advantages of our bioreactor system containing engineered Bacillus subtilis that continuously monitors the conditions of plants and releases protectants when predetermined thresholds are reached. Our system offers farmers an automatic, customizable, more effective, and sustainable way to reduce crop losses from both environmental and disease stress while lowering chemical use and improving yield reliability.

[FOLD] Current challenges and solutions
Current management methods often depend on repeated chemical spraying, manual monitoring, and frequent maintenance, leading to higher labor costs, chemical waste, environmental pollution, and inefficient use of resources. In addition, these traditional solutions are rarely tailored to the specific conditions of individual farms, making it difficult for farmers to identify the right time to intervene. By combining stress detection and treatment in a single platform, our technology reduces unnecessary chemical inputs, minimizes waste and runoff, lowers daily labor requirements, promotes faster plant recovery, and helps maintain more stable crop yields during challenging growing seasons.
[FOLD] Our solution
Unlike traditional agricultural inputs that rely on centralized chemical manufacturing and reactive spraying, our system introduces novel solutions. Instead of shipping chemical bottles to the field, we deploy an on-site, on-demand automated field factory directly to the farm. This bridges the gap left by standard solutions, which either rely on chemical inputs that cause runoff, basic biostimulants that lack automatic delivery, or passive alert sensors that identify stress but cannot treat it without human intervention. By utilizing our optimized CcaS-CcaR two-component optogenetic system linked to automated LED arrays, the bioreactor dynamically shifts production to synthesize specific protectants right at the crop site. Green and red light signals act as the trigger into the system to simultaneously regulate bacterial cell growth and control protectant production, protecting plants against both biotic and abiotic stresses without leaving chemical residues in the bioreactor culture. Furthermore, our solutions offer a cheaper solution to environmental stress when compared with other companies that offer similar options. At the moment, our prices are estimated to be at $60,000 NTD, with prices expected to decrease if our product is produced at a larger scale.
Our automated bioreactor uses engineered Bacillus subtilis to continuously evaluate crop conditions by pairing real-time field sensors with predictive stress forecasting. When environmental or biological threats pass a safety threshold, the device automatically synthesizes targeted protectants to address climate stress and plant disease precisely when and where intervention is required. Built with a flexible, modular design, the system can be adapted across different crop types and growing setups. It also takes into great consideration of biosafety, ensuring engineered bacteria remain safely contained inside the hardware to allow only active protectants to reach the crops.

Small Scale Farmers
  Our product plans to directly impact their livelihood by reducing their crop loss and health. Since our business plan aims to support and subsidise their purchases, we should keep them directly involved and informed throughout the process.

  Farmers who rely primarily on chemical fertilizer and pesticides often prioritise yield and cost. This group represents part of the smallholder farmers we are targeting. Sustainable farming farmers
  Sustainable farming farmers are the ones who prioritize reduced chemical input and soil health. Schools & Students
  Our outreach involved hands-on science education with students, introducing synthetic biology and sustainability. Although they won’t affect the development of our product, they still represent our commitment to inclusivity and long-term behavior change through education.
General Public
  We monitored public interest through interviews, workshops, and social media. Engaging with the public helped amplify our impact, though they hold limited power over the development.

### Year 1
The first phase focuses on establishing the core synthetic biology framework and achieving laboratory-scale proof-of-concept. Technical milestones include the optimization of electroporation protocols to achieve high transformation efficiency and the construction of fundamental modules such as the phycocyanobilin (PCB) module, the ccaSm10 light sensor (LSM), and the ccaR transcriptional output module (TOM). Performance is benchmarked by measuring the expression of Late Embryogenesis Abundant (LEA 14) and ACC deaminase protectants under varied light intensities (535 nm green vs. 670 nm red) to determine fold-change efficiency. Prototype systems are tested on Arabidopsis thaliana models under controlled salinity stress, measuring plant weight, germination rates, and rescue efficacy against control groups. By late Year 1 around the iGEM Jamboree, the team will demonstrate a fully integrated Machine-Microbe Interface (MMI), validating reversible, light-controlled secretion of LEA 14 and ACC deaminase within a contained bioreactor system.

## 7.Regulations¶
Taiwan presents a unique regulatory opportunity forming the foundation of our Phase 2 strategy. Taiwan has stringent restrictions against Living Modified Organisms (LMOs) and GMO field releases under the Ministry of Agriculture (MOA) and Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency (BAPHIQ). However, Taiwan’s regulatory framework contains a critical pathway: if engineered bacteria never leave the sealed device, the system can be classified as “contained-use” equipment rather than an environmental release of GMOs.
ReLeaf’s system is fundamentally cell-free delivery: non-living metabolic byproducts (protective proteins) are discharged into soil, not viable Bacillus subtilis cells. The engineered bacteria remain physically confined via the 0.22 micrometer hollow-fiber membrane with redundant genetic kill switches. By legally proving “absolute containment” to BAPHIQ and MOA, we bypass the restrictive environmental-release framework and pursue a streamlined contained-use approval pathway.
Our regulatory submission provides comprehensive biosafety validation data: escape-frequency assays, hollow-fiber membrane integrity testing, genetic kill-switch efficacy documentation, physical housing durability testing, and field trial protocols demonstrating no environmental bacterial release during pilot deployment with 20–50 devices. We partner with government-recognized testing units such as Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute (TARI) and Tainan District Agricultural Research & Extension Station to verify containment and provide regulatory credibility.

### Strategic regulatory advantage
In parallel, ReLeaf positions itself as a plant biostimulant rather than biopesticide. Under Taiwan’s Fertilizer Regulations, biostimulants follow a streamlined pathway: approximately 20 days for fertilizer specification report plus 7 days for government review equals approximately 30 days total to market. Registering as a biopesticide would require 3–5 years, multi-season field trials, GLP animal testing, and higher capital requirements. We register LEA 14 and ACCD as plant growth regulators and biostimulants under the fertilizer pathway, enabling commercial production and early revenue within 30 days, while later filing for biopesticide designation for NaD1 if regulatory circumstances and market demand warrant it.

## 8.Potential risks¶
Biological risks
Market and adoption risks
#### Genetic containment failure (CRISPR kill switch)
ProbabilityLow
ImpactHigh (regulatory rejection, loss of farmer trust)
##### Description
Engineered CRISPR-based kill switch preventing Bacillus subtilis survival outside controlled conditions could fail due to spontaneous mutation, incomplete genetic penetrance, or leaky expression, allowing engineered bacteria to survive and replicate in soil.
##### Mitigation
Rigorous testing of kill-switch efficacy under multiple stress conditions (nutrient deprivation, temperature extremes, osmotic shock) before field deployment. Quantify escape-frequency rates and document transparently. Genetic kill switch is redundant with physical and membrane containment, so failure alone would not result in environmental bacterial release. Continue monitoring throughout Phase 2 field trials with regular soil sampling around deployed devices.
#### Hollow-fiber membrane leakage
ProbabilityLow-Medium
ImpactMedium (regulatory consequence, requires containment re-verification)
##### Description
Membrane’s molecular-weight cutoff could be miscalibrated, damaged during handling, or degraded over device’s operational lifetime, allowing whole Bacillus subtilis cells (0.5–4 micrometers) to pass into environment.
##### Mitigation
Experimentally validate membrane cutoff against Bacillus subtilis cell size under multiple conditions and accelerated degradation testing under simulated field conditions (temperature cycling, moisture exposure, mechanical stress). Build membrane integrity checks into formal maintenance and replacement schedule. Conduct simple visual/functional tests monthly with farmers or extension officers.
#### Physical containment breach
ProbabilityLow
ImpactHigh (most direct pathway to uncontrolled environmental release)
##### Description
Sealed bioreactor housing could crack, degrade, or be damaged during handling, transport, or extended field use, exposing internal bacterial compartment directly to soil.
##### Mitigation
Stress-test housing for durability under realistic field conditions (moisture, temperature cycling, mechanical handling, soil microbes). Design bioreactor compartment to show visible proof of tampering or breach so any integrity failure is immediately detectable. Emphasize in farmer training and maintenance protocols that damaged devices must be returned to collection points. Redundancy with genetic and membrane containment means single breach alone is unlikely to cause bacterial escape.
#### Farmer distrust of unfamiliar technology
ProbabilityMedium
ImpactMedium (slower early market penetration)
##### Description
Farmers accustomed to familiar chemical sprays may distrust sealed biological device, especially if technology seems difficult to understand or maintain. Slow adoption in pilot phase could limit early market traction.
##### Mitigation
Clear, farmer-centric messaging avoiding overly technical language. Emphasize practical benefits: fewer spray days, reduced crop loss during heat/wet seasons, decreased daily field monitoring labor. Prioritize pilot deployments with progressive agricultural cooperatives and extension offices interested in precision agriculture and IoT systems. Generate local testimonials and case studies before broader sales.
#### Slow adoption by cooperatives and extension networks
ProbabilityMedium
ImpactMedium (distribution delays despite scientific validation)
##### Description
Cooperatives, NGOs, and government agricultural extension offices may be cautious about adopting novel agricultural inputs without long-term, multi-season evidence.
##### Mitigation
Prioritize institutions already oriented toward innovation. Design pilot trials as small, tightly controlled, outcome-focused engagements with clear success metrics: measurable crop-loss reduction, documented decrease in fungicide spray events, farmer satisfaction above defined thresholds. Deliver tangible, documented wins in first season to build credibility for longer-term scaling.
#### Production cost exceeds farmer budget
ProbabilityMedium
ImpactHigh (severely limits market penetration and farmer affordability)
##### Description
Unit cost of bioreactor device, cartridges, hollow-fiber membranes, and bacterial culture may become prohibitively expensive, limiting adoption among smallholder farmers even if technology is effective.
##### Mitigation
Comprehensive financial sensitivity analysis testing whether ReLeaf remains viable if production costs are 20% higher or selling prices are 20% lower. If direct farmer purchase is unaffordable, pivot to alternative business models: cooperative purchasing (multiple farmers share device ownership), NGO bulk purchase and subsidized distribution, seasonal rental models, or cartridge subscription systems. These flexible approaches ensure cost barriers do not prevent technology adoption.
## 9.Conclusion¶
ReLeaf represents a transformative approach to agricultural resilience in the face of accelerating climate change. By combining optogenetic control, on-site biomanufacturing, cell-free delivery, and modular design, we address a critical gap in current agricultural technology: the need for automated, adaptive, and sustainable crop protection that responds to real-time stress rather than relying on reactive, broad-spectrum chemical spraying.
Our three-layered containment approach (genetic kill switch, hollow-fiber membrane, physical housing) ensures that even if one containment layer fails, redundant protection prevents environmental release of engineered organisms. This design philosophy allows us to speak with confidence to regulators about biosafety while maintaining strategic flexibility across different regulatory environments. Our sequenced regulatory strategy of prioritizing Taiwan and Philippines in Phase 2–3 to establish proof-of-concept and build a track record, expanding to Vietnam and the U.S. in Phase 3–4, and reaching Brazil and Ghana in Phase 4+ for global scaling balances time-to-market, regulatory risk, and commercial opportunity.
Ultimately, ReLeaf’s mission extends far beyond improving agricultural productivity. We aim to support the livelihoods of climate-vulnerable farming communities, stabilize seasonal farm incomes, prevent climate-driven debt cycles, preserve ancestral land assets, and make sustainable agriculture viable for future generations. By shifting from reactive chemical management to proactive, precision crop defense, we reduce environmental contamination, protect soil microbiomes, and help establish agricultural systems that are resilient, adaptive, and sustainable for decades to come.
