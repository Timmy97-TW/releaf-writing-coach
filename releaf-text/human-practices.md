## 1.Overview¶
This page explores how our Human Practices journey has shaped ReLeaf, showing how the perspectives, experiences, and feedback of different stakeholders has influenced our decisions and help guide the project.
In our project ReLeaf, Human Practices acts as the heart of the team, connecting the research and work we have done in the lab with the real-world needs that help to shape our project. Rather than developing ReLeaf based only on what is scientifically possible, Human Practices allows us to consider how our project could fit into the agricultural world and how it may affect the people who would be interacting with it.
Sitting at the center of various topics such as synthetic biology, climate change, agriculture, and biotechnology, ReLeaf has reached out to a plethora of different stakeholders, including farmers, researchers, professors, agricultural organizations, companies, and members of the public. We hold deep value to every interaction, hoping to truly be able to gain and understand the different perspectives of differing groups of people, and to identify gaps and receive validation to further improve the quality and deepen the impact of our project.
Over the span of seven months, from March to September, our team actively engaged with 20 key stakeholders to refine and guide our project. To ensure a comprehensive and well-rounded perspective, we consulted experts across six diverse domains, including deployment, plant and stress biology, protectant design, gene circuit and cloning, bioreactor and hardware, and mathematical modeling.
This page is organized into four main sections. The Project Evolution Map provides an overview of how ReLeaf developed over time and highlights the key moments and stakeholder insights that significantly influenced our project. Expert Engagement documents our conversations and collaborations with experts from different fields, organized into individual pipelines based on different expertises and areas of discussion as well as our continuous engagement. Farmer Engagement focuses on our conversations with farmers, the primary beneficiaries of our project, and the insights we gained from understanding their experiences and needs. Finally, Public Engagement presents our efforts to communicate with and learn from the wider public. Together, these sections trace how our interactions with different stakeholders continuously shaped and refined ReLeaf.

##### BIO Asia-Taiwan Exhibition 2026
18 JulyDeploymentProtectant designBioreactor and hardware
Bio Asia-Taiwan Exhibition 2026 is a public biotechnology exhibition held in Nangang, Taipei, Taiwan. ReLeaf attended the event in order to explore novel biotechnology innovations and gain feedback on our product. Through this event, we gained insight on plant stress tolerance and the necessities for B. subtilis survival.
###### Event summary
In the BIO Asia-Taiwan Exhibition, we engaged with different companies, experts, and researchers, gaining many insights from different aspects. One of our most valuable takeaways is that plant stress tolerance depends not only on the presence of protective molecules, such as proline, but also on the processes that regulate their production and use. We also learned that dissolved oxygen monitoring and active aeration/stirring are essential for B. subtilis survival and protein/protectant output, not just passive containment.
###### Impact and adaptation
After hearing different advice from different booths, we realized that we should prioritize an O₂ sensor and feedback loop in our design. For wetlab, we can investigate irrigation-system-integrated delivery as an alternative to a standalone device and move at least some validation experiments from agar/hydroponics to soil-based trials, and increase sample size.
For business plans, we should also research the GMO regulatory landscape in Taiwan and other countries, including the Philippines, Malaysia, and EU.
###### Before
- Self-producing where AI senses stress and triggers protective release.
- On-site production avoids shelf life and transportation cost.
- An electronic sensor senses abiotic or biotic stress, goes into the microcontroller, sets off the green life, and then protectant is produced.
- Application method includes delivering the protectant to the soil through pipeline and spraying on the leaves through the irrigation system.
- Farmers only need to buy the bioreactor machine and replace the B. Subtilis powder or liquid once a year.
- Protectant candidates include ACCD, LEA14, and BoPep4.
###### After
- Utilizing agricultural waste through methods such as hay infusion for the business plan.

##### Prof. Cheng, Mei-Chun 鄭梅君
27 MarchPlant and stress biology
Cheng Mei-Chun is an assistant professor at the National Taiwan University. There, she works in the department of Biochemical Science and Technology where she specializes in Plant biology; helping us establish a plant model.
Consider shifting the plant model to Arabidopsis thaliana. Prioritize agar plate growth for early-stage control, followed by transferring to soil.
###### Event summary
Throughout the meeting with Prof. Cheng, we introduced our proposed engineered Bacillus subtilis system for detecting plant and environmental stress by producing protective outputs such as trehalose or ACC deaminase. She agreed with our ideas of choosing abiotic stresses such as heat, salinity, and drought, and choosing Arabidopsis as a suitable model for controlled experiments. Additionally, she noted that hydroponics might not be stable for our testing and suggested that we should not rely solely on it. Her expertise provided valuable guidance and helped shape and refine the direction of our project.
###### Impact and adaptation
Her feedback made us realize that experimental procedures themselves can interfere with plant stress responses. More importantly, the feedback led us to a shift toward more realistic and controllable plant-growth conditions. She suggested that the project should prioritize agar plate growth for early-stage control, followed by careful transfer to soil. We should carefully manage environmental transitions and stress timing, and use visual ROS staining to improve stress assessment. We also decided to remove osmotic stress from our targeted stresses. Additionally, Prof. Cheng kindly provided us with Arabidopsis seeds and shared valuable guidance on their cultivation and optimal growing conditions.
###### Before
- Considered heat, salinity, drought, and osmotic stresses as our stress targets
- Rely on hydroponics for proof-of-concept
###### After
- Use moderate stress conditions to prevent killing plants
- Avoid hydroponics as the main experimental system
- Move toward a plate to soil transition model instead of purely controlled systems.
- Focus more on detectable phenotypic and biochemical outputs rather than only gene-level responses.
- Potentially reconsider how the engineered Bacillus subtilis interacts with plants in realistic environments (soil vs liquid).

Dr. Paul Verslues is part of Academia Sinica and specializes in drought stress, arabidopsis research, and plant stress signaling. Work with the plant’s natural response rather than simply supplying more proline
###### Event summary
During our meeting with Dr. Verslues, we gained valuable insights into the bacterial aspects of our project. More specifically, he consulted us on the mechanisms of drought and the proline delivery system.
###### Impact and adaptation
After the meeting, we had a very meaningful takeaway on the idea of optimizing proline dosage and investigating whether bacterial products affect the plant’s natural proline-redox cycle. He also inspired us to consider testing stress priming approaches and measuring indicators such as root growth and biomass. ###### Before
- Engineered Bacillus subtilis (PGPR) detects environmental stresses (drought, salinity, heat, and osmotic stress) and responds by producing plant-protective compounds such as trehalose and ACC deaminase.
- Signal molecules (e.g., naringenin and salicylic acid) activate survival genes, improving the bacteria’s ability to persist and function under stressful conditions.
- The system enhances plant stress tolerance without genetically modifying the crop, using engineered beneficial bacteria to support plant growth and resilience.
###### After
- Explore a shift from a proline-delivery system toward a plant-regulation system, using bacterial signals or precursors to enhance the plant’s natural drought response
- Consider testing stress priming approaches and measuring indicators such as root growth and biomass

Through expert consultations, we continuously refined the bioreactor to improve containment, production, monitoring, and practical deployment.

The main menu of the LINE platform (農友助手) contains six functions: Latest News(最新消息), Product Introduction(產品介紹 ), Crop Stress Alerts(壓力警報), Crop Assistant(作物小幫手), Farmer’s Diary(農友日記 ), and Feedback(意見回饋 ). Together, these websites were designed to help farmers from receiving information and recognizing problems to finding solutions, recording observations, and providing feedback more easily.
[IMG] The six-button 農友助手 menu: latest news, product introduction, stress alerts, crop assistant, farmer diary and feedback.
#### Latest news 最新消息
The Latest News section provides users with news throughout the year, including farmer expos, farmer markets, and workshops. Information will also be posted on the website, including dates and locations.
#### Product introduction 產品介紹
The Product Introduction section introduces our bioreactor to users. By integrating the information into a brochure, users can easily understand its function and use with our slogan: “An on-site, on-demand bioreactor for smarter crop protection. Instead of explaining to users the biological and engineering mechanisms behind our project, we decided to design the website in a way that users can clearly understand at a glance.
This allows users to understand not only how our bioreactor solution works, but also how synthetic biology and agricultural technology could potentially be integrated into real farming systems.
#### Crop stress alert 壓力警報
From our interviews with the farmers, we noticed that many were triggered by environmental stress. The Crop Stress Alert section was designed into a dashboard with a weather forecast and biological risk system. The system can predict heat stress for the upcoming days and also suggest users to take action.
The website is divided into sections based on different regions of Taiwan, including Northern, Central, and Southern Taiwan, allowing users to easily assess heat stress conditions by selecting the region in which their farm is located. Each regional section contains individual blocks representing different cities. Within each block, the heat stress index is displayed on the left, while the biological risk index for crops is presented on the right.
The range of the index represents the severity of the heat stress and biological risk, with each numerical value corresponding to specific recommended actions for users.
Below the index, it will provide more information on the heat stress based on the Taiwan Central Weather Administration’s heat stress information [2]. Additional biological risk information will also be provided based on the information from the Taiwan Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency (APHIA) [3].
#### Crop assistant 作物小幫手
Crop Assistant is a website designed to support farmers by suggesting appropriate actions based on their reported farming problems and weather conditions.
Users will be required to answer the questions and report their problem in three steps, including select or entering the type of crop that is being affected, select the region where their crops are being grown. They may also enter their specific location, identify the part of the crop currently affected by the issue, such as the leaves, roots, or flowers, and describe the current weather conditions, the symptoms of the crop, the duration of the issue, the impact on the farm, and the presence of any pests. By providing these information about their situation, the website will be able to deliver a more accurate suggestion.
After completing the questions, the website will explain the causes of the issue and analyze how each factor may affect the crop, providing users with a better understanding of the problem. It will also suggest some possible solutions and prevention to this issue. If users have additional questions after reviewing the analysis, a built-in AI system will be available to provide further explanations and answer their inquiries.
However, as our website is not intended to replace professional agricultural expertise, we recommend that users consult official government resources before taking any action. Relevant government websites and resources will be provided below for users to access easily.
#### Farmer diary 農友日記
To establish a platform that enables users to communicate, exchange experiences, and share information, we developed the Farmer Diary. The platform allows users not only to share posts about their farming experiences but also to ask questions and receive responses from other users.
Each account is limited to a maximum of five posts per day. Users can select the day’s weather conditions and upload photographs of their crops along with a description. Additionally, users can record the soil moisture and provide information about pest conditions of the day.
After each post, users will earn 10 tokens, which can be exchanged for rewards, such as LINE stickers, once they reach a certain number of tokens.
There will also be a dedicated question section where users can ask questions about their problems related to crops without any posting limit. All users will be able to view and respond to these questions.
#### Feedback 意見回饋
To gather user feedback and better understand their opinions of our LINE platform, we incorporated a Google Form to collect suggestions and areas for improvement. The survey includes questions regarding users’ satisfaction with the platform, its usefulness, and potential features or functions that could be incorporated in the future.
#### Seed exchange 種子交換平台
After our visit to Tamsui Happy Farm, Ms. Chen suggested we develop a platform that would allow users to exchange seeds with one another. Based on her suggestion, we developed the Seed Exchange Platform. The platform allows users to post seed demand and supply information, including specific variety details, harvest year, and planting location. The system then matches users based on their seed requirements and availability. Once both parties confirm the proposed exchange, they can contact each other directly to arrange the exchange.
#### Auto reply 自動回覆
To help users address their questions and concerns more efficiently, we integrated an AI chatbot into our LINE account. By entering keywords related to farming issues, such as salinity, heat stress, or pests, users can receive relevant information and suggestions for addressing these problems.

#### Experts sharing
In the first section, we’ve invited experts to share their research and their experiences in farming with the other participants, including Prof. Chen, Dr. Li, and an expert from CH Biotech.
Dr. Li
Dr. Li emphasized that excessive technology intervention may address symptoms without solving their underlying causes, and compared it to the overuse of painkillers in medicine.
When a patient experiences a headache, most doctors may immediately prescribe painkillers to relieve the symptoms, while rarely examining the patient’s lifestyle or addressing the underlying problems causing the condition.
He used the example of Zambia to illustrate how external support and high-yield seeds can reduce farmers’ independence, while natural farming practices using locally adapted seeds and fewer external inputs may support more sustainable farming.
Prof. Chen
Prof. Chen highlighted that the purpose of smart agriculture is not simply to introduce technology into farming, but to combine IoT sensors and AI models to integrate farmers’ experience into data and provide early warning of potential risks.
CH Biotech
From an industry angle, the expert from CH Biotech shared that agricultural waste, such as feathers, can be used to produce signal peptides as biostimulants, which can help crops maintain energy metabolism under stresses, including typhoons and high temperatures, without increasing fertilizer or pesticide use.

#### Experts feedback
After the experts shared their research and experience, we received valuable feedback on our project and bioreactor design. Their insights helped us to further improve our design.
Dr. Li
Dr. Li encouraged us to rethink conventional agricultural practices from natural farming perspectives. He emphasized that rather than directly intervening in plants to improve their growth or resistance, creating an environment that allows plants to develop their own resilience to changing climatic conditions is important.
Prof. Chen
Prof. Chen introduced the engineering perspective of smart agriculture, focusing on how technology can be used to create a more favorable environment for plant growth. Prof. Chen also emphasized the importance of maintaining a balance between intervention and natural plant resilience and suggested that protective measures should be applied based on whether plants can continue growing under specific stress conditions. This perspective highlighted the importance of humane interactions with plants and the environment rather than excessive intervention.
CH Biotech
From CH Biotech, we learned the potential applications of bioprotectants in agriculture. The expert introduced us to a key concept: plant metabolism and homeostasis. He stated that rather than simply maximizing plant growth or continuously increasing protection, the goal should be to find a balance between growth and defense.

## 6.Conclusion¶
#### Integration of Human Practices
Human Practices was embedded into ReLeaf from day one, serving as a continuous feedback loop that helped us understand how to improve our project further. Across seven months from March to September, every stakeholder’s insights directly impacted and led to changes in research direction, plant focuses, and hardware designs, ensuring that our technical development was constantly shaped by real-world needs.
#### Implementing Our Human Practices
Our outreach was also carefully planned around specific knowledge gaps and technical challenges we have identified as time went on. We provided clear context for each stage of ReLeaf by examining existing agricultural methods, prior work, and current biotechnology limitations, helping our team understand the current global reality and where our solution could step in to relieve these issues. Every expert interview and field visit had a defined purpose, where members of the team conducted deep research regarding current roadblocks we were facing and the person that would be able to help the most with our problems. This helped with ensuring our team approached stakeholders with well-researched questions and implemented their feedback with clear rationale, making each visit targeted and with a clear goal set in mind.
#### Responsible Innovation
Throughout our project, the question of how we could design a solution that was safe and helpful remained in our minds. To achieve this we engaged in interviews and meetings with several experts such as farmers, biosafety experts, and plant biologists. All these helped ensure that ReLeaf was designed with safety, ethics, and environmental consideration at its core. By consulting specialists in deployment and gene circuit safety, we prioritized biocontainment and responsible release mechanisms, ensuring our solution actively protects crops under climate stress without introducing ecological risks. Furthermore, we have also been in contact with farmers, the primary customer of our project, to ask for their opinions and concerns to make sure the problem we are trying to solve is actually an issue that farmers face.
#### Stakeholder Engagement
Together, we gathered perspectives across a broad spectrum of 20 stakeholders across six distinct domains: deployment, plant and stress biology, protectant design, gene circuit and cloning, bioreactor and hardware, and mathematical modeling. Combining these technical insights with the lived experiences of local farmers and public feedback allowed us to balance high-level scientific rigor with practical usability in the field.
#### Documentation & Continuity
In previous pages, we have also mapped out our entire Human Practices journey on this page, including our Project Evolution Map, pipeline breakdowns, stakeholder interview summaries, and the specific design changes triggered by each interaction. Our Integrated Human Practice page records and mapped out every single one of our interactions and the specific steps we have taken afterwards. By transparently detailing our successes, challenges, and methodology, we provide an accessible, step-by-step roadmap that clearly showcases the journey we have been through as a team.
#### Broader Impact
Lastly, ReLeaf demonstrates how synthetic biology can move beyond abstract lab research to tackle urgent global challenges like climate change and food security. By showing how open dialogue with communities and experts can transform a student project into a practical, human-centered solution, we hope to inspire future researchers to build science hand-in-hand with the people it is meant to help.
