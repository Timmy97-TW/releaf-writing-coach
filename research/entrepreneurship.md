## Criteria

From `sources/text/judging-special-prizes.md`, "Best Entrepreneurship". The
specialization asks for a business case built on customer needs and expert
input on feasibility, plus a minimum viable product. Five ballot questions:

1. First potential customers, and unmet needs not covered by existing solutions.
2. Solution possible, scalable, inventive.
3. Product development plans with realistic milestones, timelines, resources, risks.
4. Skills, capabilities and stakeholders needed to be credible.
5. Positive and negative long-term impacts of the fully developed solution.

## WageningenUR 2025 OG

**Page anatomy.** Two pull quotes from named CEOs (84) · `Judging criteria`,
the five ballot questions answered (927) · `Background` (289) · `About BCoated`
(147) · `Market size analysis`, TAM, SAM with an SPA canvas, SOM (964) ·
`Competitor analysis`, direct, indirect, future, conclusion (707) ·
`Cost analysis`, investment, production, capital, with itemised sub-lists
(2,100 across sub-sections) · `Timeline` (236) · `Financial analysis`, cash flow
and NPV (283) · `Risk analysis` table (294) · `Obtaining capital`, grants and
M&A (433) · `The sustainable business canvas` (42) · `Market entry`, MVP, first
customers, patenting, expansion (822) · `Skill gap analysis` (343) ·
`Long-term benefits & disadvantages` (1,182 including references).

**How it opens.** No introduction. Two quotations from the CEOs of NanoCell
Farms and SeedForward, each attributed with name and company, one of them
saying it would find money for the product. The reader meets a customer before
meeting the team. Then a sentence of housekeeping and the five ballot questions
as headings.

**Customer and need.** The pattern is: company name, what that company told
them, what the team did next. Excerpt `wur-01`. The need is never stated in the
team's own voice alone; it is quoted to a buyer. Phrases that recur: "told us",
"has expressed interest", "We have scheduled a visit". The B2B choice is
justified from an interview finding, that farmers are cautious of new products,
and the page says the brand name of the client is what gets around that.

**Numbers.** The strongest arithmetic in the set. TAM is a market report with a
figure, a 2030 projection and a CAGR, all one citation. The CAGR is then
compared to the S&P 500 return so an investor-shaped reader can place it. SAM
comes from a scored canvas with a stated weighting and a footnote saying the
access scores came from the team's own network and from experts. SOM is written
out as a multiplication, `€640.5 million x 0.12 x 0.33 = €25.36 million`, with
the 33% sourced (`wur-03`). Cost analysis runs from kilograms of seed to
kilograms of cellulose to litres of medium to euros, each assumption labelled as
an assumption. Thirty-four numbered references. There is no price per coated
seed anywhere on the page.

**Validation.** Interviews, used as constraints and not as decoration. The RIVM
and the European Commission DG SANTE E4 Unit are each cited for an approval
duration, and those durations set dates in the timeline. A physical chemistry
expert is named for the waste-stream suggestion, and the team then grew
cellulose on waste beer yeast from a named local brewery to check it. No LOIs;
interest is reported as interest.

**Milestones and risks.** Timeline is a prose year-by-year, with the regulatory
clock written in. Risk analysis is a seven-stage table, every row a phase, a
risk and a solution. Scalability is conceded in the ballot answer itself
(`wur-02`), and the concession decides which product launches first.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural throughout. Present tense for what
exists, future for what is planned, and the two are not blurred: "we have
shown" against "we will". Conditional language where it belongs: "we anticipate",
"we will assume", "suggesting that the investment is worth pursuing".

**What it folds.** Nothing substantial. Sub-costs are given as short labelled
lists before the detailed treatment, which is a fold in effect.

**Where it is weak.** The 30% market share assumption sits inside SAM, where SAM
is defined as what the business model can address; capture belongs in SOM, so
the 30% and the later 0.12 × 0.33 are doing similar work twice. The cash-flow
analysis assumes the SOM is the revenue, which skips price and volume entirely.
A unit slip in the consumables calculation: `1,197,000,000,000 J = 1.197
GJ/year` should be 1,197 GJ, though the number used afterwards is right. The
investment-cost sentence is duplicated verbatim in consecutive paragraphs. The
Latin America row of Table 1 is missing its accessibility score. A stray `par`
survives at the start of a paragraph in the skill gap section. Buzzword load is
the lowest of the seven: four hits across 9,113 words.

**For ReLeaf.** This is the closest analogue: an agricultural input, sold B2B,
gated by EU regulation, bought by people who distrust new products. Three
things to take. Open on a customer sentence, not a mission. Write the SOM as a
visible multiplication so a judge can audit one factor. Put the regulatory clock
in the timeline and attribute the duration to the body that gave it.

## UNILausanne 2025 OG

**Page anatomy.** `Key Points Summary`, nine cards of 18 to 39 words (222) ·
`Introduction` (75) · `Our Mission` as `Why?` / `What?` / `How?` (356) ·
`Market study and identified needs` (59) · `Market Study` with a TAM/SAM/SOM
table (292) · `Our service has the great potential…` (36) · `Current Technology
Landscape` (125) · `Solution Implementation` (224) · `LCA and service-quality
testing` plus a folded explainer and a partner table (654) · `Building our
Venture` (186) · `Timeline` table (154) · `Stakeholders`, matrix plus a
nine-row table (698) · `Expected revenue` (336) · `Impact Assessment` with a
Doughnut explainer (336) · `Team’s skills and capabilities` (317) ·
`Entrepreneurship in Action`, four company profiles (511) · `Incubators` (325) ·
`References` (211).

**How it opens.** A summary card deck. Nine labelled cards, each one topic, each
under 40 words, covering technology, market, impact, certification, revenue,
stakeholders, roadmap, competition and evidence. A reader who stops after 30
seconds still has the whole business. Then a short introduction and a
Why/What/How triad.

**Customer and need.** `Why?` is the model paragraph in this file (`unil-07`):
urgent need, supply concentration, who is exposed, and a recovery rate below 1%.
The need is quantified in one clause. Customers are classes, not companies:
"Swiss and European recyclers handling magnet-rich feedstock". The nearest thing
to a named first customer is a list of potential pilot partners with their
expertise and role.

**Numbers.** TAM, SAM and SOM in kilotonnes per year, in a table with a
description column that says what each row means, plus a CAGR. The figures are
repeated as a bullet list after the table, which helps a scanning reader. No
source is attached to the TAM, SAM or SOM figures; the reference list is
thirteen items and includes Wikipedia. No cost figures, no price figures, no
headcount costs. Revenue is described in kinds (service fees, oxide sales,
sustainability premiums) with no amounts. "Initial pilot-scale sales are
projected to generate significant turnover" is the whole financial projection.

**Validation.** Four CEO interviews, each written up as a profile of the company
instead of as a finding (`REEcover`, `Soren`, `Volutio`, `Tibio`). The profiles
are informative and the insight extraction is thin: what each interview changed
is not stated, and the full summaries are pushed to the Human Practices page.
The incubator paragraph is better (`unil-10`): named adviser, named programme,
the three members who signed up, the pitch city and date.

**Milestones and risks.** A five-phase timeline table with dates and activities.
No risk analysis at all. For a ballot question that names risks explicitly, this
is a hole, and the page wins anyway on the strength of its market work.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural, present and conditional. Heavy use of
"could" and "potentially", sometimes stacked: "could potentially help us". The
conditional is honest about a pre-revenue company and it dilutes sentences.

**What it folds.** Two explainers behind disclosure triangles, one for Life Cycle
Assessment and quality control, one for Doughnut Economics. Also four company
profiles. The folding decision is sound: definitions and background fold, the
argument stays flat.

**Where it is weak.** `TAM` appears in the summary card with no expansion, and
the first full expansion is far below it (`unil-11`). The acronym is given as
"Serviceable Addressable Market" in the table and the standard term is
Serviceable Available; the summary card calls it "a serviceable market in the
EU". One paragraph ends in a consultant's second person, telling the reader
about "your REE recycling service" (`unil-08`), which is text written for
someone else and pasted in. Three different figures share one image alt string
left over from the wet lab (`pet28a-dogcatcher-8dq2-schema`). The Key Points
cards are marked up as top-level headings, so the page has about eleven h1s, and
two further headings are full sentences used as pull quotes. Section title "Our
service has the great potential to address the market's needs" is a claim, not a
name.

**For ReLeaf.** Take the summary deck. Nine cards, one per topic, under 40 words
each, is a cheap way to make a long page survive a short visit, and ReLeaf's
three markets would fit the pattern. Take the `Why?` paragraph shape. Do not
take the missing risk section, and expand every acronym at first use, including
in summary cards.

## SUSTech-BIO 2025 UG

**Page anatomy.** `1. Project Overview` (431) · `2. Project Background`, disease
burden, pathogens, bottlenecks, policy (692) · `3. Pain Points of Existing
Drugs`, including two expert interviews (993) · `4. Market Size Forecast`,
global then six regions (648) · `5. Market Analysis`, PEST then SWOT (2,944) ·
`6. Commercialization Path`, pathway choice, protectability, value chain,
strategy (2,171) · `7. Development Strategy`, STP segmentation, targeting,
positioning, marketing (2,400) · `8. MVP`, design and verification (896) ·
`9. Financial Planning`, cost assumptions, financing, cash flow (1,714) ·
`10. Risk Analysis and Response Strategies` (1,290) · `11. The 3rd iGBA Form`
(626) · `12. Business Plan`, download (9).

Map first, then read: sections 2, 3, 4 carry the need and the market evidence;
section 8 carries the MVP data; section 9 carries the costing method; sections
5, 7 and 10 are framework-driven and repeat each other.

**How it opens.** A structured abstract. One paragraph on the problem and the
platform, then labelled blocks: Business Model, R&D and Regulatory Strategy,
Licensing and Partnership Strategy, Market Strategy, Financing Plan, Strategic
Summary. Each block is two to five lines with the numbers in them. It reads like
an executive summary and it works; a reader knows the plan before page two.

**Customer and need.** The need is a clinical gap, argued well: deaths per year
with a named source, the undercoding problem, then the therapeutic drought
(`sustech-12`). The customer is the harder part. The business model is
license-out, so the buyer is a pharma partner, and the page says so plainly in
section 6.4: the team has the technology and lacks the complementary assets, so
collaboration is the route. Two signed technical development agreements stand as
customer evidence.

**Numbers.** Dense and mostly sourced inline. Market size given as optimistic
and conservative scenarios with the drivers and constraints of each listed, then
a risk disclosure quantifying three downside scenarios in CAGR points. Regional
2024 base figures for eight markets. Costing is the best part: every salary,
training cost, consulting rate and trial cost is benchmarked to a named
publication, and where a range exists the page says it takes the conservative
end. The risk-adjusted trial budget is the standout move (`sustech-14`): divide
the direct cost by a published phase-to-launch success rate and show the
division.

**Validation.** Eight or nine named experts across the page, each with
institution and role: two professors on the science, a capital research head on
the commoditisation-versus-drug decision, a BGI investment director on valuation,
an FA partner on risk, a marketing agency on channel pricing. The pathway choice
in section 6.1 is the best-written: two options laid out, the adviser's
reasoning given, the decision stated, the reason for the decision restated. Also
a letter of recommendation and two agreements as downloads.

**Milestones and risks.** Positioning gives a dated regulatory path with trial
phases, participant counts and a target launch month. Financing is three rounds
with amounts and what each round buys. The cash-flow section runs a sensitivity:
at 80% of target, years three, four and six break. Risk scoring is explicit:
impact × probability × uncontrollability on a 1 to 5 scale.

**Voice and tense.** Mostly impersonal and nominal, in the register of a
consulting report: "The project adopts", "The regulatory pathway focuses on",
"Enterprises should quantify risks". First person appears where the team did
something. The passive-nominal default makes long stretches hard to attribute:
section 10.4 is four pages of recommended measures with no stated owner.

**What it folds.** Nothing. Downloads for the confidentiality agreement,
financing cases, frequency table and business plan.

**Where it is weak.** The results block is the serious problem. One organism is
called six different things across twenty-five lines, and none of them matches
the others: `Candida acida`, `Lactobacillus acidophilus`, `Acinetobacter
citrophila`, `Aspergillus flavus`, `Citrobacter watermelonis`, `Aspergillus
citreus` (`sustech-16`). A section headed "Amphotericin B Hydrochloride Efficacy
Testing" is referred to in the conclusion as "amorolfine hydrochloride". Under
Echinocandin, the "Representative" is given as "Candida", a pathogen listed as a
drug. The cost advantage of engineered bacteria is given as 37% lower in one
section and as 17% of chemical drugs in another. A marketing objective names the
Vasculitis Foundation and "innovative cell therapies", which belong to another
project. `leverage` appears ten times, `cutting-edge` five. Section 10.4 is
generic business advice that would read identically for any company. The MVP
kill figure (99.6%) is stated as the remaining population ratio in one sentence
and as a kill efficiency above 99.9% in the next. The disclaimer, by contrast,
is the honest high point (`sustech-13`).

**For ReLeaf.** Take the structured abstract. Take the costing discipline: every
unit cost benchmarked to a named source, and the conservative end of each range
chosen out loud. Take the risk-adjusted budget move. Avoid the organism-naming
failure, which for ReLeaf would mean WB800 drifting to 168 or to "B. subtilis"
mid-page; one name, used everywhere, checked at the end.

## Uni-Padua-IT 2024 UG

**Page anatomy.** Nav list of 24 anchors (77) · `Unmet needs → Statement
problem` (277) · `Beachhead market` (93) · `Competitors` (255) ·
`Benchmarking (TAM-SAM-SOM)` (265) · `Customer interview` (369) ·
`Our Solution → SurPFAS Technology` (241) · `IP Protection Strategy` (48) ·
`Business Exit strategy` (417) · `Development Plans → Lean Business Model`,
nine canvas blocks (1,121) · `Gantt chart` (52) · `Risk Analysis` (313) ·
`Business Plan` link (8) · `Long Term Impacts → SWOT Analysis` (488) ·
`Costs` (409) · `Long-Term Development and Immediate Actions` (400) ·
`Sustainable Development Goals` (153) · `Team & marketing → Marketing and
Communication` (181) · `Marketing Strategy` (272).

**How it opens.** Straight into chemistry. What PFAS are, why they persist, the
local history (Veneto, 1964, Miteni), the health effects with a citation. No
team introduction, no mission. By sentence four the reader knows the problem is
regional and personal to the team.

**Customer and need.** The strongest need-discovery writing in the set, because
it is a site visit written as a site visit. The team watched a filter being
replaced, learned that samples go out every two weeks and come back in days,
learned that thermally regenerated carbon no longer fits the cisterns, and each
of those two facts becomes a design decision in the next sentence (`padua-18`).
Phrasing pattern: "The main issues we identified were…", "The need for X was
emphasized", "Based on this feedback, we decided to…". The beachhead is stated
as a geography and a plant size, with the expansion path after it.

**Numbers.** Mixed. TAM is a dollar figure with a citation. SAM and SOM are
given as geographies with definitions and no figures, so two of the three rings
have no size. The cost section more than makes up for it: a real desorption
ratio, a real filter bed mass, a growth-test tolerance limit carried through to
litres of dilution water and a dollar cost, ending in a verdict against the
team's own first proposal (`padua-19`). Several cost steps are images, so the
prose refers to results the text does not state.

**Validation.** One customer, deeply. Acquevenete is visited, its practice
described, its two complaints recorded, and the product changed twice in
response. Competitors are named (Chemviron, Calgon Carbon, Cabot Norit and
others) and the incumbent process is characterised accurately. No LOIs, no
survey.

**Milestones and risks.** The Gantt is an image with a one-line prose target
(industrial level by 2028). Risk Analysis is prose and good: three failure
modes, each with what it would force the team to do next, and an opening
sentence that concedes the Gantt phases are ideal.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural, active, short sentences. Present for
the problem, future for the plan. The register slips at the end: the two
marketing sections drop into promotional voice ("a young, innovative movement
created by a group of twenty-somethings", "We’re not just fighting PFAS").

**What it folds.** Nothing; everything is flat, which at 5,500 words is
workable.

**Where it is weak.** `Marketing Strategy` opens by restating the opening of
`Marketing and Communication` almost word for word (`padua-21`). "Unfair
advantage" is five adjectives and nothing checkable, and it claims a
"patent-pending" sensor and "exclusive partnerships" that are not evidenced
elsewhere on the page (`padua-20`). Skills and stakeholders, ballot question
four, are not addressed; the section called `Team & marketing` contains no team
content. Typos in headings survive: "Costumer interview", "Costumer segments",
"Strenghts". The SDG section is nine one-line positives. `crucial` six times.

**For ReLeaf.** Take the customer interview shape: observe, record the
measurement, name the design change. Take the cost verdict, including the
sentence that rules out the team's own option. ReLeaf has three target markets,
so take the beachhead discipline too: say which one is first and why, in a
geography and a customer size. Do not leave a ballot question unanswered, and do
not let the marketing voice take over the last screen.

## UToronto 2024 OG

**Page anatomy.** `About Us` (320) · `iGEM Entrepreneurship Criteria`, the five
questions answered (1,100) · four document sections, business plan, pitch deck,
one pager, cash flow (80) · `Our 2024 Demo Day Pitch` (30) · `Advisory Board`
(40) · `General Information` (60) · `Most Recent Status` (60) · then structured
company data: industries, deal history, investors, competitors, team (tables) ·
`Appendix` (40).

The shortest page in the set, and roughly half of it is tabular company data in
the format of a private-markets database entry.

**How it opens.** The weakest opening of the seven. One sentence carries
"forefront", "groundbreaking", "revolutionize", "rigorous", "transformative" and
"significant impact" (`utoronto-22`). Two real facts are inside it, two named
incubator programmes and a 10-year funding roadmap, and both are hard to find.
The second paragraph is a table of contents in prose.

**Customer and need.** One sentence does most of the work, and it is a good one:
a named San Francisco company approached the team wanting to integrate the
platform, and has sent a project proposal. Direction of contact is stated, which
is strong evidence. The same paragraph then claims the technology "flawlessly
integrates" and is "projected to cut preclinical costs by ~70%", with no source
(`utoronto-23`).

**Numbers.** Thin in prose, rich in tables. TAM, SAM and SOM exist only as an
image, and the acronyms are never expanded. The Gantt chart, risk management
chart, competitor summary, skill gap analysis and SWOT are all images. What is
textual is concrete: $15 million Series A target, a 5 to 8 year estimate, two
funding events with dates and amounts ($10,500 accelerator, $16,531 non-dilutive),
and a competitor table with founding years, total raised and last financing for
five named companies. Unusually, the competitor data is sourced-looking and
specific where the team's own projections are not.

**Validation.** One inbound customer, two named partner labs (Garton Lab, Lan
Lab), an advisory board, two incubator programmes completed, a Paris showcase
slot. Named bioethicists and a biosecurity expert for the impacts question.

**Milestones and risks.** Four short stage paragraphs, Seed through Exit, each
saying what the money buys. Risks are named as categories (market, product,
societal) with the sources of each, and the content is in an image.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural, present perfect for achievements
("we've identified and secured", "We’ve crafted"), future for plans. Two
registers coexist: promotional in `About Us`, flat and factual in
`Most Recent Status` (`utoronto-25`). The second register is better and the page
would be stronger if it ran throughout.

**What it folds.** Everything heavy: business plan, pitch deck, one pager,
cash-flow projections and a two-part appendix, all as PDFs.

**Where it is weak.** Buzzword density in the opening. TAM/SAM/SOM as an
unexplained image. Risk and skill content outsourced to images, which a judge
reading text cannot audit. Headcount is 5 in `Most Recent Status` and 6 in the
competitor table. A competitor is spelled "Ardis Pharmaceuticals" in one table
and "Aridis Pharmaceuticals" in the next. Typos in headings and body: "susiness
advisory board", "Merger & Acqusition". "Crafted using the FEEL Methodology™ of
Venture Creation, our business plan delves into…" is a trademark and a buzzword
in one clause. The honest moments are good and buried: the novelty hedge
(`utoronto-24`) and the one-line admission of a pivot due to weak
product-market fit, which sits in the last section on the page.

**For ReLeaf.** Take the ballot-question headings; a judge reading to a ballot
finds the answer in one scroll. Take `Most Recent Status` as a register model:
dated, countable, no adjectives. Take the inbound-contact sentence pattern. Do
not put evidence only in images, and do not open with six adjectives.

## Tec-Chihuahua 2023 UG

**Page anatomy.** Nav blocks (~200) · an interactive diagram whose five nodes
are the five ballot questions, with the explanatory text run together into one
paragraph (~820) · `Solution`, with a CEO pull quote, administration route,
MVP mockup (~500) · `AureoBos' Technology` (455) · `Scalability` and
`Inventiveness` (~200) · `Development Plans`, SWOT, then a nine-block
`Detailed Business Model` (1,880) · `Exit Strategy`, `Milestones & Next Steps`,
`Intellectual Property`, `Business Documents`, `Legal Considerations` (2,316) ·
`Unmet Needs`, problem statement, dairy producers, pains, first customers,
existing solutions, TAM/SAM/SOM (~2,570 with references) ·
`Skills, Capabilities & Stakeholders`, eighteen named stakeholders (~2,400) ·
`Long-term Impacts`, five positives and two negatives (~350).

The five top-level units are the five ballot questions. The 2023 UG winner
already built the page as an answer sheet.

**How it opens.** `What? / How? / Why?` in three short answers, then an
empathise-define-ideate-prototype-test narrative. The idea is good and the
execution is the page's worst writing: the design-thinking sentence has long
explanatory asides spliced into it, so one paragraph reads as a sentence
interrupted five times. The intended interaction (hover or click to expand)
collapsed into flat text, and the result is unreadable in sequence.

**Customer and need.** The best need-definition writing in the set. The unmet
need is written as the customer's own list of conditions: effective without
antibiotics so milk is not discarded, backed by veterinarians and by evidence
comparing it to conventional treatment, easy to fold into existing routines,
harmless to cattle, milk and people, and affordable in that economic context
(`tec-26`). Every item is testable against a product spec. The customer is then
split into two segments by purchasing route, with the intermediary that becomes
a customer named as a consequence (`tec-27`). The role of veterinary
zootechnicians as non-buying influencers is identified and then carried into the
channel and marketing sections.

**Numbers.** TAM, SAM and SOM built from government livestock counts, smallest
ring first, each count sourced (`tec-28`). The incidence rate that converts
herds into sick animals is cited. Then a second market figure arrives in dollars
from a market-insights vendor, and the two units, cows and dollars, are never
reconciled. The cost of mastitis is given with the population it is computed
over, which is the right way to state a market burden. No price for the product
anywhere; the only commercial detail is the pack format, 20 syringes of 10 mL.

**Validation.** The strongest stakeholder documentation of the seven. Eighteen
named people, each with role, organisation, and the specific thing they said or
changed: a technology-transfer officer on joint ownership of IP, an MSD manager
who redirected them from multinationals to local laboratories, a Zoetis
technical manager on the exit route, a state sanitary-risk commission that
pointed them to the right registration authority, judges who asked about
scalability. Plus five producers offering to test on infected cows, and a
documented award with a $5,600 grant and AWS credits.

**Milestones and risks.** Milestones are the page's strength and its oddity:
they are past events, each dated and with an outcome (pitch nights, a dairy
industry fair, an awards final). Future steps are an interactive timeline image.
Risk assessment is a download, so ballot question three is half-answered in
text.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural, warm, present and future. Heavily
promotional in places: "We are catalysts for change", "we will be revolutionizing
mastitis treatment". Register shifts a lot between sections.

**What it folds.** Most of the page, into pop-ups and clickable cards, which the
rendered text flattens. Business plan, pitch deck, risk assessment, legal plan
and trademark assessment are downloads.

**Where it is weak.** Buzzword load is the highest in the set: `pivotal` 14,
`crucial` 13, `delve` 4, `revolution` 4 across 8,584 words, and `pivotal` is
used of nearly every stakeholder. The inflated claim in `Solution` arrives
before the evidence that would have carried it (`tec-29`). The eighteen
stakeholder cards are valuable and repetitive, several ending in the same
sentence shape ("This kind of feedback…", "His guidance played a pivotal
role…"). A nav link reads "Entrepuernuship". The design-thinking opener is the
clarity failure to show students. Long-term impacts are the cleanest part:
negatives marked, caused and mitigated (`tec-30`).

**For ReLeaf.** Take the unmet-need-as-customer-conditions list; for ReLeaf,
written as what a grower or a formulator requires of a biostimulant, each line
checkable against the peptide. Take the segmentation-by-purchasing-route move
for the three markets. Take the long-term impacts layout. Do not let interactive
text become unreadable flat text, which is a direct risk for any page built on
hover panels.

## Leiden 2023 OG

**Page anatomy.** `Our mission`, an image (2) ·
`Unmet needs & potential customers` (166) · `Technology` (132) ·
`Exit strategy` (299) · `Opportunity`, three sub-sections on plastics,
regulation and clean production (556) · `Total Addressable Market` (160) ·
`Serviceable Available Market` (95) · `Serviceable Obtainable Market` (127) ·

`PHAse Out team` (125) · `Stakeholders` (76) · `Long-term impacts` (248) ·
42 numbered references (947).

Top-level headings are four of the five ballot questions, in ballot order.

**How it opens.** The cleanest opening of the seven (`tec` aside). Tonnage with
a citation, a concession that the plastics are useful, the harm with a citation
and an admission that its extent is unknown, then the gap in one sentence and
the words "this is a large unmet need" (`leiden-31`). Four sentences, three
references, one claim. The third paragraph then says who was consulted before
the need was asserted.

**Customer and need.** Need first, customers later and by name. The customer
segments section distinguishes the direct buyer (PHA manufacturers), the end
user (farmers), and three adjacent buyers, and names two real companies as
candidates. The exit strategy is where the customer logic is really settled: a
horizontal merger, because the team would not build production itself.

plastics market, takes the bioplastics share, then the PHA share of that, and
lands on a figure, each step a sentence with its own reference (`leiden-33`).
TAM is a market report with a 2021 value, a CAGR and a 2030 projection. R&D cost
is estimated from comparable companies with a source. The one formula on the
page, profit per ton of PHA, is given with no values in it. Forty-two
references, the most of the seven, and the market figures are the best-sourced
after WageningenUR.

**Validation.** Interviews used to overturn the team's own plan, which is the
highest-value use of an interview. Stakeholders told them a Dutch PHA production
company would be hard to make real, and the exit strategy changed to a merger
(`leiden-32`). A named professor's three R&D recommendations are reported,
including the one he warned might fail. An applications expert is quoted
directly on the dependence on government support.

**Milestones and risks.** Eleven key activities, each a short paragraph saying
why the step exists. Table 1 maps every risky phase to a risk and a solution,
nine rows. Funding bodies named with amount ranges and the team's own standing
with each. The risk table and the key activities use the same phase names, so
the two sections line up.

**Voice and tense.** First person plural, measured, conditional where
appropriate: "we would", "we may", "we estimate". No promotional register
anywhere. The nearest thing to a boast, "we are sure to be successful in making
PHAse Out into a profitable start-up", is one sentence at the end of the team
section.

**What it folds.** A Life Cycle Assessment definition in a folded box
(`leiden-35`), plus the business model and competition blocks.

**Where it is weak.** The `Serviceable Available Market` section defines SAM,
reasons about Europe and North America, and then ends by calling its own result
the TAM (`leiden-34`), so no SAM figure is ever stated and the TAM is given two
different values on the page. One revenue-stream sentence is cut off mid-clause:
"our revenue will stem from the direct sales of PHA to all". The TAM citation
and a sibling reference give different market sizes without the difference being
noted. Skills are claimed generically in the ballot-question section, and the
concrete content sits in `Key partners` instead. `crucial` four times.

**For ReLeaf.** Take the opening: number, concession, harm with a citation,
gap, the words "unmet need". Take the sourced chain down to the SOM. Take the
interview that changed the plan and say on the page that it changed the plan.

## Cross-case synthesis

### Shared skeleton

Five of the seven pages run on the ballot. Leiden 2023 and Tec-Chihuahua 2023
use the five questions as their five top-level units; UToronto 2024 and
WageningenUR 2025 print the questions verbatim as headings and answer them;
Uni-Padua-IT 2024 uses the question titles as section names. SUSTech-BIO and
UNILausanne use a consulting structure instead and each leaves one question
thinly answered.

Under that, a common order:

1. Problem, with numbers and citations. Never the team first.
2. Unmet need named as a gap in existing solutions, usually in one sentence.
3. Customers, from a class down to a named first customer.
4. Solution and why it is inventive, measured against named competitors.
5. Market size, TAM to SAM to SOM.
6. Competitors, split into direct, indirect, future (Leiden, WageningenUR).
7. Money: costs, revenue model, funding plan.
8. Timeline, with milestones.
9. Risks, as a phase/risk/solution table.
10. Skills and stakeholders.
11. Long-term impacts, positive and negative.
12. Downloads: business plan, pitch deck, financials.

Four of the seven put the business plan on the page and use the PDF as backup
(WageningenUR, Leiden, Uni-Padua-IT, SUSTech-BIO). Three push the substance into
PDFs (UToronto most, Tec-Chihuahua for risk and legal, UNILausanne for the
market study).

### UG against OG

UG pages are longer and more framework-driven. SUSTech-BIO runs PEST, SWOT, STP
and a value chain; Tec-Chihuahua runs a Business Model Canvas, SWOT, empathy map
and benchmarking; Uni-Padua-IT runs a Lean Canvas, SWOT and an SDG list. OG
pages carry more arithmetic and fewer frameworks: WageningenUR builds a cost
model from equipment upward and computes an NPV; Leiden chains sourced
percentages to a SOM. The one OG page that is framework-led, UNILausanne with
Doughnut Economics, is the one with no risk section.

UG pages document people more thoroughly. Tec-Chihuahua's eighteen stakeholder
cards and SUSTech-BIO's nine named experts outdo every OG page on named-source
density. OG pages are better at saying what the named source changed.

UG pages carry heavier promotional language. Buzzword counts across whole pages:
Tec-Chihuahua 41, SUSTech-BIO 24, Uni-Padua-IT 12, Leiden 9, UNILausanne 8,
UToronto 7, WageningenUR 4. The two lightest are OG; the two heaviest are UG.

### 2023 to 2025

The ballot questions became visible page furniture. In 2023 they are implicit in
the heading structure; in 2024 and 2025 UToronto and WageningenUR print them.
A page that answers the ballot in its own headings is now the default.

Market arithmetic got more auditable. Leiden 2023 already chains sourced
percentages; WageningenUR 2025 goes further and prints the multiplication as an
equation with a reference on the contested factor. Pages that give TAM, SAM and
SOM without showing the derivation (UNILausanne, Uni-Padua-IT for two of three
rings, UToronto as an image) are the weaker instances in every year.

Signed documents appeared. Tec-Chihuahua 2023 reports five verbal offers in
prose. SUSTech-BIO 2025 uploads two technical development agreements and a
letter of recommendation, and discloses that one was signed by an intern on a
verbal commitment. WageningenUR 2025 has no signed document and reports interest
as interest, with the company named; that reads as credible as the uploads.

Cost modelling moved onto the page. 2023 pages estimate R&D from comparables;
WageningenUR and SUSTech-BIO 2025 both build bottom-up cost structures with
every unit cost benchmarked to a named publication.

### Sentence patterns that make a business claim credible

Taken from the passages that survive scrutiny.

- **Name, then quote, then consequence.** "SeedForward told us that a seed
  coating to deliver insecticides to seeds is in high demand, yet no appropriate
  solution is available. Based on this information, we started working on…"
  (WageningenUR). The claim is checkable because the source is named and what it
  said is reported.
- **Visible multiplication.** "our SOM will be €640.5 million x 0.12 x 0.33 =
  €25.36 million" (WageningenUR); the four-step chain in Leiden's SOM; livestock
  counts in Tec-Chihuahua. A reader can attack one factor instead of the figure.
- **Figure with its denominator.** "Based on a global dairy cow population of
  271 million, it has been estimated that treating it costs the dairy industry
  around $19.7B–32B (USD)" (Tec-Chihuahua). A market burden stated with what it
  was computed over.
- **Stated assumption, labelled.** "We will assume that 1% all of the seeds are
  coated"; "For a conservative estimate of labour costs, we have assumed the
  upper limit" (WageningenUR). An assumption that announces itself costs nothing
  and buys the whole calculation.
- **Method before result.** "RMB 100 million ÷ 9.7% ≈ RMB 1.03 billion"
  (SUSTech-BIO). The division is on the page, so the number is a method.
- **Duration attributed to its source.** "According to European Commission - DG
  SANTE - E4 Unit… this approval will take between 2.5-3.5 years"
  (WageningenUR). Timelines become evidence when the body that owns the clock is
  named.
- **Concession that changes a decision.** "we learned from stakeholders that
  setting up a PHA production company in the Netherlands ourselves may prove too
  challenging to be realistically implementable" (Leiden); "For that reason,
  BCoated will initially focus on ex situ approaches" (WageningenUR). The
  admission earns its place by altering the plan in the next sentence.
- **Negative result published.** "As highlighted from an economic perspective,
  this alternative does not prove to be cost-effective" (Uni-Padua-IT). Fourteen
  words against the team's own proposal.
- **Hedge placed at the claim.** "Given the proprietary nature of our
  competitors' technologies, we're unable to verify how close our approach is to
  current methods" (UToronto); "To the best of our knowledge" (WageningenUR).
  The limit of knowledge sits beside the knowledge claim.
- **Flat status line.** "Employees: 5 (Entrepreneurship Team) As of September 9,
  2024" (UToronto). Dated and countable.
- **Need as customer conditions.** A list where each item is a requirement the
  product can be tested against (Tec-Chihuahua).

### Anti-patterns, in winners

- **Adjective stacks instead of evidence.** UToronto's opening; Uni-Padua-IT's
  "Unfair advantage"; Tec-Chihuahua's "revolutionizes, completely". Every one of
  these sits next to a paragraph that does the job properly, which is the
  argument against them.
- **Acronyms used before they are defined.** TAM and SOM in UNILausanne's
  summary cards; TAM/SAM/SOM as an image with no text expansion in UToronto.
- **Acronym swapped mid-section.** Leiden's SAM section concluding with a TAM
  figure. One word undoes a paragraph of arithmetic.
- **Unsourced percentages.** "projected to cut preclinical costs by ~70%"
  (UToronto); "the mass production cost of engineered bacteria is only 17% of
  the latter" (SUSTech-BIO, contradicting its own 37% elsewhere).
- **Two units for one market.** Tec-Chihuahua's cows and dollars, never
  reconciled. Leiden's two TAM values.
- **Inconsistent entity names.** Six organism names in one SUSTech-BIO results
  block; Ardis/Aridis in UToronto; headcount 5 against 6.
- **Template text from elsewhere.** "your REE recycling service" (UNILausanne);
  a vasculitis patient group in an antifungal marketing plan (SUSTech-BIO);
  section 10.4 of SUSTech-BIO, which would read identically for any company.
- **Duplicated paragraphs.** Uni-Padua-IT's two marketing openings;
  WageningenUR's repeated investment-cost sentence.
- **Framework output as the answer.** SDG lists of one line each
  (Uni-Padua-IT); SWOT bullets that restate the prose above them (most pages).
- **Evidence only in images.** UToronto's risk chart, Gantt, skill gap and
  TAM/SAM/SOM; Tec-Chihuahua's future timeline; Uni-Padua-IT's cost steps.
- **Interactive text that flattens badly.** Tec-Chihuahua's opener.
- **A missing ballot answer.** No risk section (UNILausanne); no long-term
  impacts (SUSTech-BIO); no skills or stakeholders (Uni-Padua-IT).
- **Price absent.** No page in the set states a price for its product. Five
  describe revenue models in kinds with no unit price. The cheapest available
  improvement on any of these pages.

## Rewrite checklist

Testable rules. Each one can be checked against the page in under a minute.

1. **Make the five ballot questions findable in the headings.** A judge reading
   to a ballot should reach each answer in one scroll. Leiden, Tec-Chihuahua,
   UToronto, WageningenUR, Uni-Padua-IT.
2. **Open on the problem with a sourced number, not on the team.** The reader
   needs the stake before the solution. Leiden, Uni-Padua-IT, SUSTech-BIO.
3. **Name the unmet need in one sentence containing the word "need" and a
   statement of what does not exist.** It makes the claim quotable and
   falsifiable. Leiden ("this is a large unmet need"), WageningenUR.
4. **Name at least one first customer, with what they said and who contacted
   whom.** A named company with a quoted condition beats any number of
   unattributed stakeholder mentions. WageningenUR, UToronto, Uni-Padua-IT.
5. **Write the unmet need as the customer's own list of conditions.** Each item
   should be testable against the product spec. Tec-Chihuahua.
6. **Expand every acronym at first appearance, including in summary cards and
   figure captions.** A judge may land mid-page. Leiden and WageningenUR expand
   TAM, SAM, SOM, CAGR, MVP and NPV; UNILausanne and UToronto do not.
7. **Show the arithmetic for every market figure.** Print the multiplication
   with the source on each factor. WageningenUR, Leiden, Tec-Chihuahua.
8. **Check the acronym in the last sentence of every market paragraph against
   the heading above it.** This is the exact error Leiden shipped.
9. **Label every assumption as an assumption, in the sentence that uses it.**
   "We will assume that…", "assuming constant operation". WageningenUR,
   SUSTech-BIO.
10. **Give one unit price, or say explicitly that pricing is not yet set and
    why.** No winner does this, and it is the gap a judge will notice first.
11. **Attribute every duration to the body that controls it.** Approval times,
    patent grant times, trial lengths. WageningenUR (DG SANTE, RIVM),
    SUSTech-BIO (NMPA, CDE).
12. **Make every risk row a phase, a risk and a named response.** No risk
    without an owner's next action. WageningenUR (seven stages), Leiden (nine
    rows).
13. **Include at least one limitation that changes a decision on the page, and
    say which decision.** WageningenUR (in situ scale-up), Leiden (exit
    strategy), SUSTech-BIO (the patent disclaimer).
14. **Publish at least one result that goes against the team.** One sentence is
    enough. Uni-Padua-IT (the cost verdict), UToronto (the pivot).
15. **Give both a positive and a negative long-term impact, each with a cause
    and a mitigation.** Tec-Chihuahua (five green, two red), WageningenUR (two
    and two).
16. **Say what evidence you do not have before claiming novelty.** Put the
    hedge in the same sentence as the claim. UToronto, WageningenUR ("to the
    best of our knowledge").
17. **Use one name per entity, everywhere, and check it at the end.** Strain
    name, company name, product name, headcount. SUSTech-BIO and UToronto both
    failed this.
18. **Put the evidence in the running text.** Images support text; they do not
    replace it. WageningenUR and Leiden pass, UToronto fails.
19. **Keep every section's first sentence unique.** Read the opening sentences
    in a column and delete repeats. Uni-Padua-IT shipped two near-identical
    marketing openings.
20. **Cut adjective stacks and count the buzzwords.** Four hits in 9,000 words
    (WageningenUR) against 41 in 8,500 (Tec-Chihuahua), both winners; the
    cleaner page reads as the more credible one.
21. **Fold definitions and background; keep the argument flat.** Explainers
    behind a disclosure triangle, decisions and evidence in the running text.
    UNILausanne (LCA, Doughnut), Leiden (LCA).
22. **Write one flat status paragraph: dated, countable, no adjectives.** It is
    the register the whole page should be able to fall back to.
    UToronto's `Most Recent Status`.
