# Sustainable Development Impact casebook

Prize: Best Sustainable Development Impact (slug `sustainability`). Six winners,
2023 to 2025, read from `sources/text/sus-*.md`. Marburg 2024
(`mar-2024-sustainability.md`) is read as a comparison only; it did not win this
prize. Excerpts for the mock page are in `sustainability.excerpts.json` (35
passages, every one checked against its source file).

Word counts below are counts of the rendered text, rounded. They include
captions and reference lists where noted.

## Criteria

The 2026 description asks teams to evaluate their project against one or more
SDGs, consult SDG stakeholders, start collaborations with other iGEM teams
around the goals, and revisit earlier iGEM projects and build on them. Judges
answer five ballot questions. The table shows where each winner answers them.

Two parts of the written criteria are almost unanswered by the winners. None
builds on a named earlier iGEM project. Only Thessaloniki shows work with other
iGEM teams around the goals (a shared Instagram video). Marburg 2024, which did
not win, is the only page in the set that names earlier teams (Keystone 2021,
Brasil-USP 2015). This is open ground for ReLeaf.

## EPFL 2025 (UG)

Project: drone-applied biopolymer hydrogel against wildfires. About 2,500 words.

### Anatomy

1. Overview (220): the wildfire problem from two sides, then four SDGs with one design rule each
2. 10 Reduced Inequalities (145): statistic banner, framing paragraph
   - Stakeholders (260): Thailand, Burkina Faso, Brazil, Canada, Australia
   - Long-term Impact (190): six bold-lead bullets
3. 13 Climate Action (70)
   - Stakeholders (130)
   - Long-term Impact (410): seven bold-lead paragraphs, cited
4. 14 Life Below Water (95)
   - Stakeholders (100)
   - Long-term Impact (330)
5. 15 Life On Land (80)
   - Stakeholders (180)
   - Long-term Impact (235)

Every goal has the same three parts: a one-line statistic with its source, a
paragraph on why the goal fits, then Stakeholders and Long-term Impact.

### Opening

It starts with the problem. "We examined both sides of the
wildfire problem": the fires, and the fire-fighting chemicals. The four goals
then arrive as design rules ("Prioritize affordability...", "Favor
low-toxicity, low-runoff formulations..."). A reader knows by word 200 what the
team will build and what it will avoid.

### Choosing SDGs

Four goals, justified by the problem. Targets are named only twice (13.1 and
13.3, in the last paragraph of SDG 13). SDG 15 mentions "a core SDG 15 target"
without a number. The page works at goal level.

### Trade-offs

Thin. "Avoiding burden-shifting" is one bullet under SDG 10, and the overview
ends "without shifting burdens from one domain to another." The real trade-offs
appear inside stakeholder entries: BC Wildfire still prefers fixed-wing
aircraft; NAFC warned that small drones carry small payloads over vast
distances. The team narrows its use case in response. That is the strongest
limitation on the page, and it is not labelled as one.

### Quantifying impact

- Proposed indicators: "responder injuries, deployment time in low-resource
  districts, cost-per-hectare protected, and adoption rates." No baseline, no
  target.
- Unit-matched comparison: aircraft "up to 65 kg CO₂ per flight hour" against
  drones "less than 0.1 kg CO₂ per hour".
- Literature LCA: "60–70% reduction in production energy" from Tunley
  Environmental and ETH Zurich. The team's own process is not measured.
- Own lab tests are reported as "significant reduction in burn rate and in
  surface temperature" with no number on this page.

### Stakeholders

The best stakeholder entries in the set for a short page. The Mirror
Foundation entry runs: what they showed (leaf blowers, satellite alerts on
phones), the gaps they named (few helicopters, little protective gear, scarce
drinking water), and the design response (drones that complement ground crews).
SDG 14's entry is weaker: "Across our conversations, many organizations..."

### Voice and tense

First person plural. Past tense for what stakeholders said, present for the
design. Two slips into second person under SDG 15: "shapes how you design and
deploy our product" and "guidance you used".

### Folds

None in the rendered text. Each goal is visible in full.

### Weak points

- Lab to field in one sentence: lab tests "leading to a decrease in CO₂
  emissions per wildfire event in field applications." No field data exists on
  the page.
- An unexplained metric: "over 90% of organic matter intact post-fire compared
  to 55% in untreated zones." No method, no source for whose test this is.
- SDG 14 Long-term Impact is mostly general writing on ocean pollution; the
  hydrogel appears in two of seven paragraphs.
- The Fundo Casa entry is repeated almost word for word under SDG 14 and SDG 15.
- The quote "cause their own harm, damaging ecosystems" is used twice with no
  speaker named.

### What ReLeaf can take

- Give every SDG the same three parts: why it fits, stakeholders, long-term
  impact. Judges find the answer to ballot questions 1 and 2 in the same place
  each time.
- End each stakeholder entry with the design consequence.
- Propose indicators a farm could count (for example yield under drought,
  input cost per hectare, adoption by cooperatives), and add a baseline and
  target, or say there is none yet.
- Keep the lab result and the field expectation in separate sentences. This
  matters for ReLeaf: the plant screen supports the stress assay and has not shown a rescue.

## Aalto-Helsinki 2025 (OG)

Project: AbsorBuddy, a possible oral vitamin B12 supplement for people with
pernicious anaemia. About 3,950 words.

### Anatomy

1. Note: "This section is not meant to be read separately."
2. What is Sustainability? (700): definitions, Brundtland, three pillars, SDG critique
3. Our project in relation to Sustainable Development (330)
4. Good Health and Wellbeing (470): Targets 3.8 and 3.4; SDG 3 description box (100)
5. Quality Education (240): Target 4.4; description box
6. Reduced Inequalities (390): Targets 10.2 and 10.3; description box (80)
7. Responsible Consumption and Production (820): Targets 12.4, 12.5 and 12.6; description box (90)
8. Limitations and potentials tensions with other SDGs (450): SDG 8 (8.4), SDG 16 (16.6), SDG 9 (9.4)
9. References (190)

### Opening

A 700-word essay on what sustainability means, before the project is named.
It is well read and well cited, and it is the wrong first screen for a
project page. The second section does the real opening work: it names the
product, the four goals, the stakeholders, and the project's stage, and says
the page offers "critical reflections" and makes no promise that the solution
is sustainable "in every way."

### Choosing SDGs

The most target-precise page in the set after UZurich. Each goal section opens
with the target's text, then a paragraph on how the project meets it. Four
goals claimed, three more named as tensions.

### Trade-offs

The model section in the set. "Limitations and potentials tensions with other
SDGs" names three goals at target level, and for each says what the team has
not done: no market research or pricing (SDG 8), no contact with policymakers
or regulators (SDG 16), no life-cycle thinking for scale-up (SDG 9). It closes
by calling the trade-offs "a first step toward mitigating this unbalance."

### Quantifying impact

One measured number: "we quantified the plastic waste generated by our
experiments (~ 4.4 kg)." The page explains why it did not do more: stakeholder
discussions moved the team "from unrealistic and speculative life-cycle
modelling of an unscaled supplement toward a realistic assessment of our
laboratory footprint." That sentence turns a missing LCA into a deliberate
method choice.

### Stakeholders

Described in groups (patients, doctors, civil society, researchers). Almost no
names on this page. The one concrete policy finding ("one injection every three
months" rule) is reported as a result of Human Practices research.

### Voice and tense

Academic, hedged ("has potential to contribute", "If proven safe and
effective"). Some self-assessment the judges would rather make themselves: "Our
project also demonstrates a clear awareness of the interconnections among
SDGs."

### Folds

The SDG description boxes are click-to-expand ("click to read more"). They
repeat UN text and could go.

### Weak points

- The essay opening.
- Target 8.4 is quoted as "improve global resource efficiency in consumption
  and production" beside an argument about affordability and access. The target
  does not fit the claim.
- Target 12.4 is quoted as "By 2030". The UN text of 12.4 says "By 2020"; check
  before copying target wording.
- "SGD 4" in the intro.
- SDG 4 claims the outreach work "directly contribute[s] to equipping youth
  with technical and scientific skills essential for future employment" with no
  numbers.

### What ReLeaf can take

- State the project's stage and what the page will not claim, early.
- Quantify the team's own lab footprint (plastic mass, hazardous reagents,
  autoclave runs). It is measurable this season, unlike field impact.
- Write a tensions section with a target number, the risk, and the work not
  yet done, one paragraph per goal.
- Cut the definitions essay; one sentence on what the SDGs are is enough.

## UBC-Vancouver 2024 (UG)

Project: nuCloud, enzymatic DNA synthesis (TdT) for DNA data storage, with a
low-cost bioreactor and microfluidic chips. Main page about 8,200 words
including 660 of references. Two subpages: Green Chemistry (about 4,000,
including 720 of references) and Green Engineering (about 1,275).

### Anatomy

Main page:

1. Contents, Overview (65): five SDGs "as discussed below"
2. SDG 13 Climate action (1,740): Problem Identifications, Problem 1, Problem 2, Long Term Social, Environmental, and Economic Impacts, Solution to Problem 1, Solution to Problem 2, Feedback from Relevant Stakeholder, Positive/Negative Interactions with Other SDGs, Links to Documentation
3. SDG 12 Responsible Consumption and Production (1,980): same template, four problems and four solutions
4. SDG 9 Industry, Innovation, Infrastructure (1,200)
5. SDG 4 Quality Education (1,140)
6. SDG 8 Decent Work and Economic Growth (1,060): adds "nuCloud's Potential Negative Impacts in Addressing SDG #8" and "Linkage to other SDGs"
7. References (660)

Green Chemistry: Overview, Goal and Definition Scope (goal, functional unit,
boundaries), Life Cycle Inventory (chemical and enzymatic, three tables), Life
Cycle Analysis, Life Cycle Improvements, Limitations (150).

Green Engineering: Overview, SDG 8, 9 and 12 paragraphs (about 125 each),
Performance Comparison: Culturing Density, Culturing Efficiency, Cost,
Electrical Usage.

### Opening

Flat: project name, one line of method, "our project aligns with SDGs 13, 12,
9, 8, and 4." It works because the template that follows is so regular.

### Choosing SDGs

Five goals, each with targets (13.2, 12.2, 12.4, 12.5, 9.1, 9.4, 9.5, 9.a,
9.b, 8.1 to 8.5, 8.a). Some fit loosely: Target 13.2 is about national policy,
and it is used for a technology that saves electricity and water.

### Trade-offs

The only winner with a positive/negative interactions heading under every
goal. Strongest passage: the SDG 8 negative-impacts section, which says the
sequencer requirement "could hinder the inclusive economic growth advocated by
Target 8.a, despite us previously claiming to be aligned with it." Green
Chemistry weighs bleach against autoclaving as a real trade-off.

### Quantifying impact

The most own numbers of any winner, and the clearest model for ReLeaf because
the subject is a bioreactor:

- Cost: "approximately CA$60 for the materials" against an Incu-Shaker Mini at
  "CA$5,100".
- Power: "8W" against "200W". The page says "nearly 20 times less power"; the
  ratio is 25.
- Growth: "0.430 per hr" against "0.169 per hr", with the metric defined
  first.
- Density: about three times higher after six hours (from OD600).
- Inventory: "950 mL of SOC", "1.695 Kg of plastic", reagent volumes in mL and g.
- Literature: E-factor of "4700 Kg of waste per 1 Kg of product" for chemical
  synthesis.

### Stakeholders

Four named sources (Rashmi Prakash, Karin Strauss, Nikita Telkar, Let's Talk
Science coordinators). Entries are long and conversational. Nikita Telkar's
entry is the best: she asks the team to "quantify the metrics our product will
address," and the page points to where the comparisons are. Rashmi Prakash's
conversation is retold under two goals, with her surname spelled two ways.

### Voice and tense

First person plural, informal ("it is no secret", "can be purchased through
Temu"). Frequent spelling errors.

### Folds

None. Detail moves to subpages: the LCA and the hardware comparison.
That keeps the main page's argument readable and gives the evidence its own
address.

### Weak points

- The same quantity disagrees with itself. SDG 13: DNA storage "~15 USD per
  terabyte". SDG 8: "800 million USD per terabyte", with 15 USD as the price of
  tape.
- Water arithmetic does not hold: "about 300,000 gallons of water a day" per
  data centre and 5,381 US centres, then "3 million, to five million gallons of
  water a day ... just in the USA."
- "416 terawatts" for annual data-centre electricity (energy needs TWh).
- Two references read "TODO" and "find source".
- Spelling: "tradditional" (4), "harzardous" (3), "coolign", "esitmate",
  "exponetial".
- Problem sections are mostly literature on data centres; the project appears
  late in each goal.

### What ReLeaf can take

- Compare the farm-scale bioreactor with a commercial unit in the same units:
  build cost, power draw, yield per run. UBC's Green Engineering page is the
  template.
- Use a fixed per-goal template whose headings echo the ballot questions.
- Put the LCA-style inventory on its own page and end it with a limitations
  list.
- Recompute every ratio and check every repeated number before freeze.

## UZurich 2024 (OG)

Project: RhyzUp, an engineered Pseudomonas sp. IsoF root biofilm for drought
tolerance, pathogen resistance and nutrient uptake. About 5,300 words
including 910 of references. The closest project to ReLeaf in the set: a
microbe for crops, Swiss farm stakeholders, fertilizer and pesticide questions.

### Anatomy

1. Introduction (155)
2. GOAL 2: ZERO HUNGER (1,810): Target 2.4 split into 1) drought (Switzerland, Ethiopia, global), 2) plant pathogens, 3) fertilization
3. GOAL 3: GOOD HEALTH AND WELL-BEING (680): Targets 3.2 and 3.9
4. GOAL 6: CLEAN WATER AND SANITATION (640): Targets 6.3 and 6.4
5. GOAL 13: CLIMATE ACTION (390): Target 13.1
6. GOAL 15: LIFE ON LAND (550): Targets 15.1, 15.5 and 15.8
7. References (910)

Each goal opens with a large statistic ("600 Million", "974.14%", "1/3",
">500,000") and its source.

### Opening

Method first. The team studied what makes an agrosystem sustainable, through
desk research and interviews with "agroecologists, farmers, ethicists, and
lawyers." One sentence then ties three research themes to what the product
does. The SDG list comes last.

### Choosing SDGs

Every heading is a target, and every target is quoted in full. Target 2.4 is
split into three mechanisms, each tied to one product function. This is the
clearest SDG-to-mechanism mapping in the set.

### Trade-offs

No negative-interactions section. Two passages do the work partly:

- Target 15.8 treats the engineered strain as a possible invasive species and
  lists containment (xylose sensing, kill switch, microgranule delivery). A
  stakeholder adds that commercial rhizobacteria must be endemic.
- Under fertilization, a stakeholder explains that the Swiss reduction path
  lowers protein content and selling prices. That is an SDG 2 / SDG 8
  interaction, though the page does not name it as one.

### Quantifying impact

Context only. National and global statistics are cited well, but the page gives
no number for RhyzUp itself. Claims such as "Using symbiotic rhizobacteria, as
we do, is very cheap and easy to implement" have no figure behind them.

### Stakeholders

Woven into the argument by name and role: Rahel Emmenegger (SGPV) on which
regions and crops face drought; Mosisa Wakjira on Ethiopian rainfall regimes;
Fritz Meier and Vivienne Oggier on when pesticides cannot be avoided; Reto
Flückiger on approval rules. Entries are specific (Lake Geneva to Schaffhausen;
the Rhone Valley; rapeseed). The weak point is the step from input to decision:
the stakeholders inform the context more than they change the design.

### Voice and tense

Plain explanatory present, first person plural. Confident to a fault:
"directly contributes" or "directly addresses" five times. One slip into second
person: "Thinking about the ecological consequences of the use of your projects
is crucial."

### Folds

None.

### Weak points

- "RhyzUp leads to a decrease in the use of pesticides" under Target 6.3, after
  the page's own stakeholders said pesticides are sometimes the only option.
- Statistic banners that do not fit the project's places: "974.14%" (Saudi
  Arabia's freshwater withdrawals) heads a goal argued through Switzerland and
  Ethiopia.
- Target 15.1 carries a 2020 deadline; the page notes it has passed, which is
  honest.
- Reference [11] repeats its author string and has no title; the under-five
  mortality figure cites [19], a biofilm review.

### What ReLeaf can take

- Use targets as headings and split a big target (2.4 for ReLeaf) into one
  section per product function.
- Treat the organism's own risk as an SDG 15 question: containment of WB800 in
  and around a farm reactor, with the measures listed.
- Ask farm stakeholders where and for which crops, and print the answer.
- Avoid the pesticide trap: for ReLeaf, "reduces fertilizer use" or "protects
  yield" needs "could" until a field number exists.

## Thessaloniki 2023 (UG)

Project: Euphoresis, a pectin–chitosan hydrogel from food waste carrying
microbes for soil restoration after wildfires. About 7,050 words.

### Anatomy

1. Overview (85), Which goals did we decide to work with this year? (95)
2. Goal 15 (820): epigraph, Why goal 15?, Positive long-term impact, a professor's view, Implementing the SDGs (school visit, beach clean-up)
3. Goal 12 (900): food-waste feedstock, Boroume, waste-exchange app
4. Goal 13 (830): four expert entries
5. Goal 4 (1,150): school games, relay race, conference, a teacher
6. Goal 17 (510): iGEM video collaboration
7. Negative impact (455)
8. Impact on other SDGs (45), Goal 14 (230), Goal 3 (275)
9. Holistic Impact: webinar, Sustainability Handbook, conference and workshop steps (1,400, including references)

### Opening

Brundtland definition, the 2015 adoption, "we focused on five of them." The
second paragraph names the project's aim (soil restoration, desertification,
forest regeneration) and says the team predicted "positive and negative
effects" with stakeholders.

### Choosing SDGs

Targets are named for every goal and listed without argument. Goal 15's targets
(15.3, 15.4, 15.1, 15.2) sit in one long sentence with no link from any target
to evidence.

### Trade-offs

A full "Negative impact" section, the earliest in the set. The SDG 12 and SDG
15 parts are good: production cost at scale, a harmful chemical step in pectin
extraction, and a forestry professor who was sceptical. The SDG 4, 13 and 17
parts are weak ("discussion may lead to disagreements").

### Quantifying impact

None for the product. Activity numbers only: a 100-metre beach clean-up with
items categorised and logged, four groups of eight students, ages 5 to 18 at
schools.

### Stakeholders

Many names, mostly endorsements ("very supportive", "cheered our effort").
The one entry that changed the design is the food-waste feedstock, shaped by
Boroume and PectCof. The forestry professor's objection is reported without its
content.

### Voice and tense

Narrative past, warm, sometimes emotive ("swept them off their feet"). Grammar
slips in headings ("How does euphoresis contributes to SDG 12?").

### Folds

None. Every activity is told in full on the sustainability page.

### Weak points

- An epigraph quote opens every goal (John Muir, Mother Teresa, Barack Obama,
  Elizabeth Warren, Helen Keller).
- Unnamed sources: "We approached entities... They all agreed after a wildfire
  that O 2 levels drop as CO2 increases."
- Titles and names shift: "Mrs. Conrad Landis" then "Mr. Landis"; "Mrs.
  Stefanos Katsoulis" then "Mr. Katsoulis"; "Dr. Phoebe Koudouri" then
  "Dr.Kountouri".
- Figure numbers repeat (Figure 5, 8 and 12 twice; a late "Figure 1"), and one
  caption describes a different image.
- Unclear sentence: the project "can contribute to the limitation of global
  temperature rise below 2°C while average global temperatures are increasing
  beyond 3°C."
- Education activities (a 200-word relay race) belong on the Education page.

### What ReLeaf can take

- Tie an SDG to a material decision. If ReLeaf's medium or feedstock has a
  waste-stream option, that is an SDG 12 claim a reader can check.
- Write the negative-impact section per goal, and give each dissenting voice
  its objection and its consequence.
- Leave the epigraphs out.

## Heidelberg 2023 (OG)

Project: ReMixHD, a Pseudomonas co-culture bioreactor for upcycling mixed
plastic waste. About 9,800 words, the longest page in the set.

### Anatomy

1. Title, Ban Ki-moon epigraph, intro (150): one sentence per SDG; inclusivity work; tracking study
2. Four "Click to learn" tiles (SDG 9, 11, 12, 13)
3. SDG 12 (580): Did you know?, targets 12.4, 12.5, 12.8, Spotlight, Feedback / Our learnings ×2, Benefits, Risks
4. SDG 11 (615): same template; target 11.6
5. SDG 9 (1,060): targets 9a and 9b, stakeholder globe (eight countries), four feedback pairs, Benefits, Risks
6. SDG 13 (350): target 13.3, one feedback pair, Benefits, Risks
7. Country panels (1,930): Kenya, China, Colombia, Laos, Malaysia, Vietnam, Nepal, Namibia; each has local waste management, the stakeholder, and their feedback
8. Bioplastics (380), Hearing impairment (1,190), Woman in Science (635), Waste Export (355)
9. Empty placeholders: "Problem 2", "Stakeholder 1" to "Stakeholder 3" twice
10. Tracking mixed plastic waste (420): why, impact per SDG, battery choice, study design, reflection, first results; Results by December 14th, 2023 (110) and folded references

### Opening

One sentence per goal, each with the project's role in it: infrastructure for
countries with different waste systems (SDG 9), municipal mixed plastic (SDG
11), upcycling (SDG 12), lower CO₂ than current methods (SDG 13). Clear, after
an epigraph that adds nothing.

### Choosing SDGs

Targets quoted in full for each goal. Two fit poorly: 12.4 still carries its
2020 deadline, and 13.3 (education and awareness) heads a section whose claim
is lower CO₂ emissions.

### Trade-offs

The best risk list in the set, hidden in folds. Waste pickers could lose income
(SDG 8), offset by new technical jobs. The reactor runs at 30 °C, yet "energy
supply ... could still be a challenge in regions with unreliable power." Rural
regions may be left behind because the system needs maintenance and money. GMO
scepticism may limit sales of the upcycled product.

### Quantifying impact

The tracker study is the one piece of the team's own measured data: "Out of the
9 trackers we released in Cardiff, 5 could be traced," the rest went silent,
and all stayed in the UK by 14 December. The page says "we calculated the CO2
emitted as part of a drylab project" and gives no figure here.

### Stakeholders

The "Feedback" / "Our learnings" pair is the clearest loop-closing device in the
set. The best pair: feedback that mechanical recycling cannot handle mixed
plastic led the team to drop a PET-only design for a co-culture. Another: a
Vietnamese stakeholder's 100-dollar ceiling for a household reactor led to "a
centralized application" for communities.

### Voice and tense

Two registers. Headlines sell ("We help to upcycle mixed plastic waste to
high-quality molecules!"); body text is plain. Under the Colombia and Nepal
panels, text headed "Feedback on implementing ReMixHD" is written in the
team's voice, so stakeholder input and team claims blur.

### Folds

Benefits and Risks are folded under every SDG; country panels sit behind a
clickable globe; references are folded. A judge who skims sees the headlines
and the feedback pairs and misses every risk.

### Weak points

- Template placeholders left on the live page ("Problem 2", "Stakeholder 1"...).
- "1.5 trillion people worldwide are affected by hearing loss" (the WHO report
  says 1.5 billion).
- Colombia panel: "targeting 12% of overall recycling within the next two
  years," with no baseline or source, under a stakeholder heading.
- "By this, the problem will be solved" (SDG 11 learnings).
- Hearing impairment (1,190 words) names no SDG; Woman in Science names SDG 5
  once. Together they take nearly a fifth of the page.
- "Columbia" for Colombia; "prize" for price.

### What ReLeaf can take

- The Feedback / Our learnings pair, kept short: one sentence of input, one of
  change.
- A risk list per goal that names who bears the cost. For a farm bioreactor:
  energy supply, maintenance skills, upfront price, and who on the farm loses
  or gains work.
- Ask stakeholders for a price ceiling and print it.
- Keep risks unfolded. Fold the country detail.

## Marburg 2024 (comparison)

Not a winner of this prize. Natural rubber from dandelion. About 1,200 words of
prose. Three sections at goal level (8, 12, 13 and 15 together), no targets,
one stakeholder (Stefan Hörmann of Fair Rubber), all numbers market or
literature context. "We believe" or "We strongly believe" seven times.

What it does well: two honest limits (mechanised harvest "may threaten the
livelihood of current producers"; dandelion rubber "does not solve" the rubber
waste problem), and it names earlier iGEM teams that worked on rubber
degradation, which no winner does.

What separates it from the winners: no targets, no stakeholder entry per goal,
no number about its own work, and no section on SDG interactions. It shows
what the winners added: precision at target level, a stakeholder behind each
goal, and at least one measurement.

## Cross-case synthesis

### Shared skeleton

All six winners use the same frame:

1. A short intro that names the project and the chosen goals (3 to 5 claimed).
2. One section per goal, each with: why this goal (often with one context
   statistic), the target(s), how the project contributes, stakeholder input,
   long-term impact.
3. A place for negative interactions: per goal (UBC, Heidelberg), as one
   closing section (Aalto, Thessaloniki), or almost nowhere (EPFL, UZurich).
4. Links to evidence pages or subpages.

The goal section is the unit. Judges read down a column of goals and check the
same five questions in each.

### UG and OG

In this sample (three of each):

- The undergraduate pages carry more of the team's own activity and hardware
  numbers (UBC's bioreactor, Thessaloniki's outreach, EPFL's drone comparison)
  and more spelling and consistency errors.
- The overgraduate pages argue more: Aalto's tensions section, UZurich's
  target-by-target mapping, Heidelberg's eight-country stakeholder set. Their
  English is cleaner.
- Both tracks won with weak spots. The prize does not require polish; it
  rewards a page where every ballot question has a findable answer.

### 2023 to 2025

- Pages got shorter: 7,000 to 9,800 words in 2023, 5,300 to 8,200 (plus
  subpages) in 2024, 2,500 to 3,950 in 2025.
- Decoration fell away. 2023 pages open goals with epigraph quotes, "Did you
  know?" boxes, exclamation headlines and click prompts. By 2025 none remain.
- Activity reports moved out. 2023 pages retell outreach events in full on the
  sustainability page; 2025 pages mention them in a sentence.
- Honesty became explicit. Aalto 2025 states the project's stage and what the
  page will not claim; EPFL 2025 proposes indicators it has not yet measured.
- Target-level claiming became standard from 2024.

### Turning an SDG claim into a measurable one

Patterns the winners use, with their source:

1. Same unit, side by side: "the Incu-Shaker Mini consumes 200W ... our
   bioreactor operates at just 8W" (UBC).
2. Own inventory with a unit: "we quantified the plastic waste generated by our
   experiments (~ 4.4 kg)" (Aalto); "1.695 Kg of plastic" (UBC).
3. Count with its denominator, failures included: "Out of the 9 trackers ...
   5 could be traced" (Heidelberg).
4. Metric defined before its value: "defined as the average rate of OD600
   increase for the third of the experiment" (UBC).
5. Present gap in numbers, then an outside roadmap: "800 million USD per
   terabyte ... tape storage costs approximately 15 USD per terabyte ... $1 per
   TB by 2030" (UBC).
6. Stakeholder threshold as a number: "no higher than around 100 Dollar"
   (Heidelberg).
7. Proposed indicators a reader could count: injuries, deployment time,
   cost per hectare, adoption (EPFL). Add baseline and target to finish it.
8. Conditional claim when nothing is measured: "If proven safe and effective,
   an oral alternative could replace or reduce the frequency of injections"
   (Aalto).

### Anti-patterns seen in winners

- Target mismatch: Aalto quotes 8.4 for an affordability point; Heidelberg
  uses 13.3 (education) for emissions; UBC uses 13.2 (national policy) for
  resource savings. SDG 13 has no target for a product's own emissions, so
  every team that claims SDG 13 strains for one.
- Lab to field in one sentence: EPFL's burn-rate tests become CO₂ savings "in
  field applications"; UZurich's pathogen data becomes "leads to a decrease in
  the use of pesticides."
- "Directly contributes" and "directly addresses" with no evidence attached
  (the word "directly" appears 8 times on UBC's page, 6 on Aalto's and
  Heidelberg's, 5 on UZurich's).
- Context statistics standing in for impact: banner numbers about the world
  (UZurich, EPFL, Heidelberg) and nothing about the product.
- Numbers that disagree with themselves: UBC's DNA storage cost; UBC's "nearly
  20 times" for a 25-fold ratio; Heidelberg's "1.5 trillion".
- Unnamed or generic sources: "entities", "organizations", "they all agreed"
  (Thessaloniki); "Across our conversations, many organizations" (EPFL).
- Team claims under a stakeholder heading (Heidelberg, Colombia and Nepal).
- Risks folded out of sight (Heidelberg).
- Off-topic sections on the sustainability page (Heidelberg's hearing
  impairment; Thessaloniki's game rules).
- Leftovers: placeholders, "TODO" references, names spelled two ways.

### Four findings for ReLeaf

1. The per-goal unit with fixed parts is the format that wins. UBC even names
   its subheadings after the ballot questions. A judge should find the
   stakeholder, the long-term impact and the negative interaction for each goal
   in the same place.
2. Own numbers are rare even among winners, so one honest measurement stands
   out. UBC's bioreactor cost and power figures and Aalto's 4.4 kg are the
   models. ReLeaf can measure its reactor and its lab footprint this season.
3. Negative interactions are where winners differ most. Aalto, UBC and
   Heidelberg name who loses and which target suffers; EPFL and UZurich barely
   do. For a farm bioreactor the obvious ones are energy, upfront cost,
   maintenance skill and containment.
4. No winner builds on an earlier iGEM project or a named collaboration around
   the goals, though the 2026 description asks for both. A short section doing
   this would answer a criterion the past winners left open.

## Rewrite checklist

Each rule can be checked on the page. Team names show where the rule is
demonstrated, or broken (marked "against").

1. **Name the project and its stage within the first 100 words of the page.**
   Judges score the project; a definition of sustainability earns nothing. UZurich, EPFL;
   against: Aalto.
2. **Claim each goal at target level, with the target number in the heading or
   first line.** A goal is too broad to judge; a target is checkable. UZurich,
   Aalto, UBC.
3. **Check that each quoted target matches the claim beside it.** A judge who
   looks up the target sees the mismatch at once. Against: Aalto (8.4),
   Heidelberg (13.3), UBC (13.2).
4. **Copy target wording from the UN text, deadline year included.** Altered
   wording reads as careless. UZurich notes the lapsed 15.1 date; against:
   Aalto's 12.4.
5. **Give every claimed goal at least one named stakeholder: name, role, what
   they said, what changed.** This is ballot question 1. EPFL (Mirror
   Foundation), Heidelberg (PET to mixed plastic), UZurich (Emmenegger);
   against: Thessaloniki.
6. **Report at least one stakeholder who limited or disagreed, with the
   objection and the consequence.** Dissent with a response is stronger than
   praise. EPFL (BC Wildfire, NAFC); against: Thessaloniki (Papaioannou, no
   content).
7. **Keep stakeholder words and team claims under different headings.**
   Readers need to know whose claim it is. Against: Heidelberg (Colombia,
   Nepal).
8. **For each goal, write one negative interaction: who loses, how, and which
   goal or target is hurt.** This is ballot question 3. Heidelberg (waste
   pickers, SDG 8), Aalto (SDG 16), UBC (8.a, 8.5).
9. **Do not fold the risks.** A skimming judge must see them. Against:
   Heidelberg.
10. **Every number about your own work has a unit and a comparison in the same
    unit.** A bare number cannot be judged. UBC (8 W vs 200 W, CA$60 vs
    CA$5,100), EPFL (65 kg vs 0.1 kg CO₂ per hour).
11. **Define a metric before you report it.** Readers cannot check a ratio
    they cannot reconstruct. UBC (OD600 rate over two hours).
12. **Report counts with their denominator, failures included.** Partial counts
    look selected. Heidelberg (9 trackers, 5 traced).
13. **Put the measured result and the expected impact in separate sentences;
    use "could" for the second.** This keeps lab results from reading as field
    results. Aalto ("If proven safe and effective"); against: EPFL (CO₂ "in
    field applications"), UZurich (pesticides).
14. **Make every repeated number identical and recompute every ratio.** One
    contradiction undoes the paragraph around it. Against: UBC (15 USD vs 800
    million USD per TB; "nearly 20 times" for 25).
15. **Limit world statistics to one sentence per goal; spend the rest on your
    project.** Context describes the world; judges look for your effect on it. Against: UZurich banners, UBC problem
    sections, EPFL SDG 14.
16. **If you propose indicators, give a baseline and a target, or say you do
    not have them yet.** An indicator without either measures nothing. EPFL
    (indicators without baselines).
17. **Write a limitations paragraph that names each gap and why it matters.**
    It tells judges how far to trust the rest. UBC Green Chemistry, Aalto.
18. **Publish one reusable thing and link it: data file, protocol, inventory,
    template.** This is ballot question 4. UBC (plastic CSVs), Aalto (4.4 kg
    benchmark), Thessaloniki (waste-exchange app).
19. **Replace "directly contributes" with the mechanism and its evidence.** The
    phrase claims what the paragraph should show. Against: UZurich, UBC.
20. **Before freeze: no epigraphs, no exclamation headlines, no placeholders,
    one spelling per name.** These cost trust and were gone from winners by
    2025. Against: Thessaloniki, Heidelberg, UBC.

## Source quality

- The text files hold the visible page. Aalto's SDG description boxes are
  truncated ("click to read more"); Heidelberg's folds are present but fold
  titles run together in the render ("Possible CO2 emission in other processes
  Skeptism against GMO's").
- EPFL's reference list is not in the text file; only inline "See reference N"
  markers remain. Excerpts stop before those markers.
- Heidelberg's stakeholder globe is interactive; the country panels appear
  twice in the text (short card, then full panel).
- UBC's reference list contains "TODO" and "find source" on the live page; this
  is in the team's source; it is no extraction error.
- Word counts include headings and captions and are rounded.
