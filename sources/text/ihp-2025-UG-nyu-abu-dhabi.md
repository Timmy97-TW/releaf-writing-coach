# Human Practices
## Overview
Most research starts with a problem to solve. Ours started with a person to know. Revitalyze began with an unusual privilege and an immense responsibility: someone very close to us has PMM2-CDG. She wasn't a theoretical patient in a distant hospital or an abstract case study in literature. She was sometimes in the lab with us. This proximity transformed how we approached every decision, experiment, and conversation. Human practices and safety weren't peripheral considerations—they became the foundation of our work. When your end user can look you in the eye, when you witness the daily reality of the disease you're trying to address, you don't rush to convenience. You deliberate with care.
This personal connection drove our three-month ideation period—one-third of our total development time. We explored and ultimately abandoned multiple project concepts, not out of indecision, but out of responsibility. PMM2-CDG is an ultra-rare disease with a limited knowledge base. Each potential direction required extensive consultation to evaluate feasibility, safety, and genuine patient benefit. We consulted protein engineers about enzyme inhibition, only to discover unacceptable risks from manipulating multi-pathway metabolic enzymes. We explored biomanufacturing approaches for metabolite supplementation, only to encounter cellular toxicity and clinical evidence of ineffectiveness. Each abandoned idea represented weeks of work—but when the life you're trying to improve is present, you prioritize getting it right over getting it done quickly.
PMM2-CDG exists in a scattered landscape. We couldn't get in touch with a single CDG specialist in the country. Our search expanded internationally to find the researchers, clinicians, and families who could guide us. The CDG community is small and tight-knit—every connection matters. We saw our role not just as researchers, but as connective tissue. Between researchers in France, Mexico, and the United States. Between clinicians managing patients in isolation. Between families navigating rare disease journeys without support.
The families we met reinforced this every day. Parents who had waited years for a diagnosis. Caregivers exhausted from experimental remedies. Children limited not just by disease, but by systemic barriers in awareness and access. Their stories taught us our responsibility extended beyond therapeutic development—to address the diagnostic odyssey, education gaps, and isolation compounding the burden.
This integrated approach shaped our RARE framework: Reflective Discovery, Adaptive Innovation, Responsive Implementation, and Ecosystem Empowerment.

### 1.1 - Mapping the CDG Landscape: Finding Needles in Haystacks
#### The Challenge of Rare Disease Outreach:
Identifying stakeholders within the Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation (CDG) community proved to be one of our greatest challenges. As a rare disease, CDG affects only a small number of individuals worldwide, making it difficult to locate patients and experts. Despite extensive research and outreach, we were unable to contact a single CDG specialist within the UAE. This revealed a major regional knowledge gap and emphasized the importance of awareness for rare disease and research.
To move forward, we expanded our scope to international outreach, contacting researchers, clinicians, and advocacy groups across continents. Many attempts went unanswered, but every successful connection felt like a milestone. Through persistence, we connected with leading CDG specialists, researchers, patients, and advocates, who offered invaluable insights into the medical, emotional, and logistical realities of living with CDG.
This experience taught us that building relationships in the rare disease space requires patience, empathy, and persistence. The difficulty in finding specialists reinforced the importance of our broader mission to raise awareness of CDGs, promote scientific understanding, and help bridge the gap between patient families and the research community within the UAE and beyond. By the end of our outreach phase, we had built an invaluable network of CDG stakeholders who not only shared their expertise but also guided our project’s direction to fit the needs of the community.
#### Campus CDG Awareness Survey
To understand awareness levels in our own community, we conducted a campus survey of 97 participants. Only 9 (9.2%) had heard of CDG before, quantifying the education gap we needed to address. However, over 80% expressed interest in learning more about rare diseases. When asked about preferred learning formats, 25% requested infographics and posters, approximately 35% preferred short video content, and over 40% requested on-campus events and workshops—insights that directly shaped our educational outreach approach.
#### Geographic Inequity
Our consultations revealed stark global disparities. Dr. Iván Duncker, Scientific Advisor at CDG CARE, described returning to Mexico in 2006 to find zero documented CDG cases despite the country's population of over 120 million. Patients surely existed but remained invisible without diagnostic infrastructure. His work establishing transferrin screening protocols and building alliances with neurologists illustrated how rare diseases require deliberate infrastructure building. Regions lacking genomic databases struggle to interpret unknown genetic variants, creating additional barriers beyond initial diagnosis.
### 1.2 - The Fundamental Challenge: Limited Patient Data
Dr. Giuseppina Andreotti articulated the central paradox facing CDG research:
“The biggest problem in CDG research is, fortunately or unfortunately, the lack of patients.”
This scarcity reflects not an absence of affected individuals, but a vicious cycle—lack of awareness prevents diagnosis, lack of diagnosis means no patient data, lack of patient data stalls research, and lack of research perpetuates absence of awareness.
This cycle constrains therapeutic development. Insufficient patient data limits validation approaches, clinical trial capacity, and biomarker development. Research directions become defined by what's feasible with minimal samples rather than what's scientifically optimal.
Understanding this limitation shaped our experimental strategy. We couldn't generate patient-derived cell lines ourselves or access large patient cohorts. This led us to Dr. Tamas Kozicz at Mount Sinai, whose lab possesses patient-derived fibroblasts and established PMM2 enzyme activity assays. This partnership provided a validation pathway for our bacterial EV approach while working responsibly within resource constraints—contributing foundational knowledge that could be rigorously tested in authentic disease models through collaboration with centers that have patient access.

### 2.1 A Journey of Pivots: Putting Patients Before Projects
When your end user is in the lab with you, adaptability becomes paramount. Each pivot we made represented months of work redirected—not out of failure, but out of responsibility. We learned that willingness to change direction when stakeholder feedback reveals better paths is a commitment to the families counting on us.
### PIVOT 1: From PMI Inhibition to Man-1-P Supplementation
#### Initial Approach
Traditional mannose supplementation for PMM2-CDG has shown limited efficacy. Our initial concept was to inhibit Phosphomannose Isomerase (PMI), which converts mannose-6-phosphate (Man-6-P) to Fructose-6-phosphate (Fru-1-P). By blocking this enzyme, we hypothesized that all the Man-6-P produced would be available exclusively for PMM2 to convert to Mannose-1-Phosphate (Man-1-p). Though PMM2 would still be faulty and inefficient, this would tip the reaction kinetic balance in favor of Man-1-P production—fixing a leak in the existing therapeutic approach.
#### What We Learned
We consulted protein engineering researcher Dr. Jin Montclare for guidance on our PMI inhibition strategy. She raised substantial safety concerns we had underestimated: PMI is involved in multiple biological pathways beyond mannose metabolism, creating potential for harmful cascading effects across interconnected metabolic networks. Blocking this enzyme risked unintended consequences we couldn't predict or control. She questioned whether direct Man-1-P supplementation might bypass these risks entirely while achieving our goal more safely. This reframed our thinking—instead of manipulating a fundamental metabolic enzyme with uncertain downstream effects in an already vulnerable patient population, we could supplement the desired product directly.
#### The Pivot
We abandoned PMI inhibition and redirected toward Man-1-P biomanufacturing and supplementation. This pivot taught us to prioritize patient safety over technical elegance and exemplifies Reflective Discovery—questioning our assumptions—and Adaptive Innovation—willingness to restart when feedback reveals safety concerns.
### Man-1-P Supplementation: A Promising but Problematic Path
#### Rationale for Man-1-P Supplementation
We were aware of a company developing a Man-1-P supplementation therapeutic utilizing liposomes as its delivery mechanism. We decided to contribute to this ongoing therapeutic effort by biomanufacturing Man-1-P to make the approach more cost-effective and accessible. Current commercially available Man-1-P costs $140 per 10 mg, a significant barrier to treatment accessibility. We explored bacterial production as a potentially more economical alternative to chemical synthesis.
#### Growing Concerns
As we developed our biomanufacturing approach, multiple consultations revealed mounting challenges. We consulted Prof. Wael Rabeh, an enzymology specialist, who raised concerns about cellular toxicity from phosphorylated sugar accumulation and the complexity of pathway networks where reverse enzymes might convert Man-1-P back to Man-6-P.
We later consulted Dr. Hicham Alaoui, Chief Scientific Officer at Glycomine, who validated our cost concerns—his company manufactures Man-1-P in-house specifically due to high commercial costs. However, he confirmed that Man-1-P has a minute-scale half-life in blood due to enzymatic degradation by circulating enzymes, presenting severe delivery challenges. He also noted that purification would be more complex from bacterial systems than from reaction vessels. During discussions with our PI, Dr. Andras Gyorgy, about metabolic engineering strategies and delivery mechanisms, we explored various approaches, but the fundamental obstacles remained: purification limitations, delivery instability, and uncertain efficacy.
Most critically, our conversation with Dr. Andrew Edmondson revealed his concerns about the neurological targeting ability of any solution to CDG, as these are the earliest and most concerning symptoms of CDGs. He added that the current sugar molecule delivery systems have produced unconvincing evidence of their ability to pass the blood-brain barrier, raising a major concern about the efficacy of our approach.
### PIVOT 2: From Man-1-P Supplementation to Enzyme Replacement Therapy
#### The Critical Question
We consulted chemist Dr. Giuseppina Andreotti, who specializes in PMM2 protein chemistry, to evaluate our Man-1-P supplementation approach. She expressed skepticism about its comprehensiveness, noting that PMM2-CDG's effects likely extend beyond simple metabolite deficiency to include developmental impacts. Most importantly, she advocated for pharmacological approaches that stabilize the mutated PMM2 protein itself, asking a paradigm-shifting question: “Why supplement the missing product when you could restore the factory producing it?”
#### The Pivot and New Direction
We abandoned Man-1-P supplementation after recognizing that we were addressing symptoms rather than the root cause. Combined with Dr. Edmondson's worry about neurological targeting and the numerous production and delivery obstacles, the case for pivoting was overwhelming. We redirected to enzyme replacement therapy—delivering functional PMM2 enzyme to restore the fundamental biological function that is deficient in patients. This demonstrates our commitment to Adaptive Innovation—using expert feedback to fundamentally reconsider our therapeutic paradigm.
Inspired by Dr. Andreotti's "fix the factory" paradigm, we now faced a new question: how to deliver this enzyme effectively? We returned to the drawing board seeking the most cost-effective, user-friendly, and efficient delivery approach. The answer: bacterial extracellular vesicles (bEVs).

### 2.2 Developing the bEV-Delivered Protein Approach
#### Delivery Strategy and Chassis Selection
We considered delivering both mRNA and protein, but ultimately decided on protein delivery to avoid post-transcriptional modification complications and achieve more direct, reliable enzyme delivery. For detailed reasoning,see our Project Design page.
The critical question became chassis selection and delivery optimization. We consulted Dr. Steven Biller, a member of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles bacterial EVs Task Force, who recommended E. coli strains with modified lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to reduce immunogenicity—directly addressing our primary biosafety concern. He provided protocols for purification methods, including density gradient centrifugation for high-purity characterization. Dr. Edgar Gonzalez-Kozlova, a computational biologist with EV experience, and Dr. Richard Steet, who works with CDG disease models, provided valuable protocols for EV isolation using size exclusion chromatography and guidance on targeting considerations for neural tissue—the primary concern for families dealing with neurological symptoms.
We also consulted drug delivery specialist Dr. Loganathan Palanikumar, who provided characterization protocols for assessing EV morphology, size, and stability, while cautioning about immunogenicity concerns with bacterial materials—reinforcing the importance of modified LPS strains and rigorous characterization.
#### Construct Design
Our Dual Delivery Approach—ssTorA-fused PMM2 and PMM2 with Tat signaling sequence—emerged from conversations with Dr. Gyorgy and research associate Dr. Andres Felipe. This design creates an elegant, fail-proof solution that works for both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria with minimal metabolic load, ensuring robust delivery regardless of chassis variations.
This journey through two major pivots demonstrates the essence of Adaptive Innovation: using stakeholder expertise to question assumptions, redirect approaches despite invested effort, and converge on solutions that address root causes. Each consultation revealed considerations we had underestimated, and each pivot brought us closer to a therapeutic approach that genuinely serves the families we aim to help.

### 3.1 - Delivery Mechanism: Addressing Real Patient Needs
Our conversations with families revealed a practical barrier we hadn't initially considered: many CDG patients struggle with feeding difficulties. Lisa described Malcolm's ongoing challenges with eating, while Karen recounted years of managing Dominic's nutrition through a G-tube due to persistent feeding problems. These realities meant that oral supplementation routes—the most convenient delivery method for most medications—were compromised or entirely unavailable for many patients. Traditional pill-based or solid medication delivery becomes problematic when the patient population has fundamental difficulties with eating and swallowing.
This insight fundamentally shaped our delivery approach. We developed a probiotic delivery strategy using engineered E. coli nissle 1917 administered via milk or similar drinks. Milk is familiar, accessible, and often easier for children with feeding issues to consume than pills or concentrated liquid medications. The probiotic approach transforms treatment from something children must struggle through into something that integrates naturally into their daily routine.
This delivery mechanism design exemplifies Responsive Implementation— By grounding our approach in the actual challenges families face daily, we aimed to create a therapeutic approach that could genuinely fit into their lives with ease.
### 3.2 - Adaptive Walking-Assistance Device
During our conversation with Ibrahim Chehade, he described mobility challenges faced by CDG patients that require extra walking training and assistance. While effective walking aids exist, he identified significant design flaws: excessive bulk makes them difficult to transport in cars or on flights; front sections limit children's reach and arm movement, discouraging active engagement; and bottom elements frequently strike children's ankles during use, leaving them bruised. These practical barriers transform assistive devices from enablers into obstacles.
Inspired by these insights, we began prototyping an adaptive walking aid that addresses these specific concerns. Our design prioritizes portability through collapsible construction, eliminates front barriers to encourage natural arm movement and reaching, and removes ankle-striking components through careful engineering of the base structure. The device aims to support balance and coordination development while accommodating the cerebellar dysfunction common in PMM2-CDG without restricting the child's natural movement patterns.
This initiative is a work in progress. We are continuing to refine the prototype through iterative design and plan to incorporate feedback from CDG families to ensure the device genuinely improves daily life. With an expected prototype date of late November, This work exemplifies our commitment to addressing patient needs beyond molecular therapeutics—recognizing that quality of life improvements require attention to the practical, physical challenges families navigate every day.

#### Initiative 2: bEV and ERT Regulatory Guide
Our conversation with Dr. Tamas Kozicz about validation pathways prompted us to seriously consider commercialization and clinical translation of our therapeutic approach. This led us to examine regulatory requirements for bacterial EV-delivered enzyme replacement therapies—and revealed a surprising gap. While individual regulations existed across multiple agencies and documents, no compiled, accessible resource guided researchers through the complete regulatory landscape for this novel therapeutic class.
As undergraduates exploring cutting-edge delivery mechanisms, we found ourselves navigating fragmented information across FDA guidance documents, ICH guidelines, and regional health authority requirements. When we expanded our scope to include UAE regulations for potential regional deployment, the complexity multiplied with halal certification requirements (UAE.S 2055-4 standard), orphan drug pathways, and emirate-specific approval processes.
Recognizing that future iGEM teams and early-stage researchers would face identical challenges, we created a comprehensive regulatory guide spanning both United States (FDA/CBER frameworks) and United Arab Emirates (MOHAP/EDE systems) requirements. The guide addresses enzyme replacement therapy development pathways, bacterial extracellular vesicle therapeutic classification, GMP manufacturing standards, clinical trial authorization processes, and pediatric considerations under PREA and ICH guidelines.
Key sections include:
- Non-clinical study requirements per ICH M3(R2) with specific considerations for enzyme deficiency models
- Phase I-III clinical trial design for rare disease therapeutics
- Bacterial EV classification challenges and precedent cases (Bexsero vaccine, dendritic cell exosomes)
- Manufacturing quality standards including endotoxin removal and potency assay development
- UAE-specific requirements: halal certification process, fast-track orphan designation (15-day review), and regional disease prevalence patterns
- Comparative analysis highlighting 21 CFR Part 600 versus Federal Law No. 4/1983 pathways
- Currently approved ERTs in both jurisdictions providing regulatory precedent
This resource transforms scattered regulatory information into a strategic roadmap, enabling researchers to design compliant development programs from inception rather than discovering requirements retrospectively. By documenting our regulatory learning process, we aim to accelerate future therapeutic development while ensuring patient safety remains paramount.

### 4.2: Resources for CDG families
#### Initiative 5: Region Physician Directory
The Problem:
One of the main challenges to early diagnosis and treatment is the fact that CDG experts are are scattered globally. Additionally, patients in underserved countries often lack access to testing and treatment facilities. Dr. Dunker noted how commonly used diagnostic methods, like mass spectroscopy, are inaccessible to most. To address this issue, Dr. Dunker developed transferring testing facilities. Event though these facilities are more accessible, they still have limited reach.
According to Dr. Dunker, some countries report zero CDG cases simply because the condition isn't being diagnosed. Consequently, many adults live with undiagnosed CDG, leaving patients and their families confused and uncertain about where to seek help.
These challenges inspired us to create the CDG Directory—a reference document that helps families locate CDG experts near their geographic locations. Something we hope will reduce one significant barrier to accessing appropriate care.
#### Initiative 6: CDG CARE Partnership
Our partnership with CDG CARE (Community Alliance and Resource Exchange) represents a cornerstone of our team’s global engagement and patient-centered approach.
Organization Background:
CDG CARE is a family-founded patient advocacy organization dedicated to improving the lives of individuals affected by congenital disorders of glycosylation through education, advocacy, and research support. The organization operates with the backing of the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative and under the scientific guidance of Dr. Eva Morava-Kozicz and Dr. Christina Duncker, with Dr. Duncker also serving as the Scientific Advisor and Director of Research for CDG CARE.
Collaboration Elements:
Through this collaboration, Dr. Duncker joined our project as an ongoing scientific advisor, providing critical feedback on our research design, patient impact assessment, and clinical relevance. He also served as an expert speaker at our physician conference, helping bridge communication between clinicians and researchers. CDG CARE also supported our outreach by sharing key resources and facilitating network expansion across their patient and professional communities.
Together, we are building a dynamic ecosystem where scientists, clinicians, and families collaborate meaningfully, integrating patient voices directly into research and development.
Reciprocal Relationship:
This relationship is deeply reciprocal. CDG CARE provides expert validation, access to clinical and patient networks, and mentorship, while our team contributes by expanding outreach and awareness in the MENA region, connecting new stakeholders to the global CDG community.
Importantly, this collaboration is designed for sustainability beyond the iGEM timeline. Our mutual goal is to continue coordinating research efforts, sharing insights, and promoting inclusive engagement in rare disease innovation well into the future. We want to ensure that patient advocacy and scientific discovery evolve hand in hand.

### Limitations, Future Directions, and Conclusion
Our journey through human practices revealed both what we accomplished and what remains beyond our reach as an undergraduate team. We successfully engaged 22 stakeholders across four continents, pivoted our project twice based on safety and efficacy feedback, and launched multiple ecosystem initiatives. Yet some limitations temper these achievements.
Experimental Validation: Our partnership with Dr. Kozicz at Mount Sinai established a validation pathway using patient-derived fibroblasts, but timeline constraints prevented completing these crucial experiments. The enzyme activity assays and glycosylation measurements that would demonstrate our bEV-delivered PMM2 approach's efficacy remain future work. We designed constructs and optimized delivery mechanisms, but proof-of-concept in authentic disease models awaits execution.
Device Development: Our adaptive walking-assistance device remains in prototype stage. While Ibrahim's feedback about ankle-striking elements and portability barriers informed our design, we are yet to receive a constructed prototype for testing—the essential validation that would transform sketches into genuinely useful assistive technology. Iterative refinement with actual patients is necessary before this tool can meaningfully improve daily lives.
### Future Directions
Immediate Next Steps include completing validation experiments with patient fibroblasts, conducting walking assistive-device user testing with CDG families through our CDG CARE partnership, and expanding the physician directory to additional underserved regions. Our physician conference model can be replicated annually, creating sustained educational infrastructure.
Long-Term Vision encompasses clinical trial pathways for bEV-delivered enzyme replacement therapy, regional biomanufacturing capacity. building to address cost barriers, and policy advocacy. The regulatory guide provides frameworks; future teams must navigate actual approval processes. Our educational materials require impact studies tracking whether early awareness genuinely shortens diagnostic odysseys.
### Conclusion: The Connective Tissue Role
We began this project with unusual proximity to our end user—a privilege that transformed every decision into a responsibility. That closeness taught us that human practices aren't a separate component to document, but the foundation that makes research meaningful. The RARE disease paradox—where lack of awareness perpetuates lack of diagnosis, data, and research—cannot be solved by therapeutics alone. It requires building bridges between scattered stakeholders, creating educational infrastructure, establishing diagnostic networks, and empowering communities. Our contribution extends beyond laboratory work to the connective tissue role we envisioned: linking researchers across continents, connecting clinicians with isolated patients, bringing families together for mutual support, and translating complex science into accessible education.
This work continues. The partnerships we forged with CDG CARE, the networks we initiated through our physician conference, the educational materials we created—these outlast our iGEM timeline. Future researchers will use our regulatory guide. Families will find specialists through our directory. Children will learn empathy through Milo's story. And perhaps, years from now, the foundation we laid will contribute to someone's earlier diagnosis, better treatment, or simply feeling less alone in their rare disease journey. That would be success worth the deliberation, the pivots, and the responsibility of having our end user present in the lab.
In the end, we want to acknowledge, and sincerely express our gratitude towards, the wonderful human beings that helped us create Revitalyze . From researchers expressing candid thoughts about our proposed solution(s) to CDG families trusting and opening up to us about their day-to-day lives–the NYU Abu Dhabi iGEM will forever be thankful to those that helped us create a difference.
