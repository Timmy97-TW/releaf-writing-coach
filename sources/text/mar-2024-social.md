Social Consequences

Map of Engagement Network
Through the crucial insights we received from Professor Dr. Dirk Prüfer at the beginning of our project we wanted to not only emphasize an alternative to traditional rubber sources through its scientific potential but also the socio-economic implications for communities dependent on rubber tree cultivation.
Sharing his thoughts about these aspects Prof. Prüfer highlighted the need to supplement, not replace, current rubber production, ensuring that new solutions do not disrupt existing livelihoods of rubber farmers. His commitment to ethical practices, including compliance with the Nagoya Protocol, inspired a more comprehensive and responsible approach to the project, integrating socio-economic and environmental concerns.
Thus, we reached out to the NGO Fair Rubber e.V. and interviewed Stefan Hörmann to gain a deeper understanding of the ethical and social challenges surrounding natural rubber production. Concerns about the harsh working conditions faced by rubber farmers prompted outreach to Fair Rubber e.V. seeking insights into their efforts to promote fair pay and sustainable practices. The goal was to learn how Fair Rubber ensures ethical production and to explore ways to incorporate these insights into their project, which aims to enhance rubber supply chain sustainability while supporting small farmers.
But we wanted to get to know more programs that support local rubber farmers by providing help to farmers improve yields and livelihoods. For this reason we contacted Jonas Dallinger from the German Agency for International Cooperation (GIZ) to gain insights into the social, environmental, and sustainability challenges in natural rubber production. Dallinger, involved in projects focusing on improving rubber cultivation in Indonesia, provided valuable information on how small farmers face vulnerabilities like low income, climate change, and challenges from new regulations such as the EU's deforestation-free policies. This perspective helped the project explore how introducing alternative rubber sources, like dandelion, could complement traditional rubber without harming farmers' livelihoods.
To understand the historical and environmental role of Hevea Brasiliensis we interviewed Mark Nesbitt from Kew Gardens. Nesbitt emphasized the complexity of plant histories, to explore the environmental and social impacts of rubber cultivation. His insights highlighted the importance of acknowledging the colonial roots of rubber plantations and the shift in research focus from large-scale agriculture to supporting smallholder farmers. This helped shape the project's inclusion of historical and ethical considerations related to rubber production.
Beyond the rubber tree we wanted to reflect on the ethical aspects of the Nagoya protocol and the ethical responsibilities of research projects:
Dr. Susanne Heim played a crucial role in the project by highlighting the importance of ethical reflection and interdisciplinary collaboration in scientific research. As a historian with expertise in the moral dimensions of rubber production, she emphasized the need for scientists to engage with the social and historical context of their work. Heim encouraged outreach to Kazakhstan, the origin of the Russian dandelion, and the inclusion of local perspectives in the research. Her insights shaped the project's approach, fostering a broader, more socially responsible perspective on the ethical impact of dandelion-based rubber alternatives.
The reason for contacting Kamila Magzieva and Sulushash Magzieva from the NGO KZ Rubber was to gain insights into the social aspects of dandelion-based rubber production, particularly in Kazakhstan, the origin of the Taraxacum kok-saghyz (TKS) plant.
The interview provided valuable perspectives on the economic and sustainable potential of TKS in Kazakhstan and the importance of adhering to the Nagoya Protocol, which governs the fair and equitable sharing of genetic resources. Their input helped shape the project's ethical approach and global context.
