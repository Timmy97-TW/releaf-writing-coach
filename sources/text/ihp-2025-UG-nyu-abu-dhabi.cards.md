## Dr. Jin Montclare — Navigating Early Project Pivots
Card title: Navigating Early Project Pivots: Insights from Dr. Jin Montclare
Date: 28 January 2025

### Summary
Dr. Montclare's technical assessment of our initial PMI inhibition approach revealed critical safety concerns and feasibility gaps that prompted a fundamental project pivot away from enzyme inhibition toward alternative therapeutic strategies.

### Rationale
Dr. Jin Montclare is a protein engineering researcher who specializes in customizing artificial proteins for human disorders, drug delivery, tissue regeneration, and nanomaterial development. During our initial topic selection phase, we consulted her to evaluate the scientific merit and safety of our proposed approach to inhibit Phosphomannose Isomerase as a strategy to improve PMM2 efficiency in CDG treatment. Her expertise in protein engineering and pathway manipulation made her ideally positioned to assess the technical viability of our enzyme inhibition concept.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Montclare raised substantial concerns about inhibiting PMI due to its involvement in multiple biological pathways, warning of potential harmful side effects. She advised working with documented proteins rather than de novo development given our expertise level, and questioned whether direct Man-1-P supplementation might be more effective than targeting Man-6-P, potentially bypassing pathway competition issues entirely. She emphasized identifying safety models for enzyme inhibition and utilizing structural studies, while noting that L-to-D amino acid replacement remained infeasible. Her critical questions about supplementation strategies and competing pathways challenged our fundamental approach.

### Integration
This meeting fundamentally redirected our project trajectory. Dr. Montclare's safety concerns about PMI inhibition—particularly the enzyme's multi-faceted role across biological pathways—made us realize this approach risked introducing unforeseen consequences that could harm patients. Her suggestion to consider direct Man-1-P supplementation opened an entirely new avenue we hadn't explored. We ultimately abandoned the PMI inhibition strategy, recognizing that manipulating such fundamental metabolic enzymes without extensive safety data was irresponsible for a rare disease population with limited treatment options. This pivotal consultation taught us to prioritize patient safety over technical novelty.

## Prof. AJ Afzal — Exploring Bioinspired Filtration
Card title: Exploring Bioinspired Filtration: Consultation with Prof. AJ Afzal
Date: 29 January 2025

### Summary
Prof. Afzal's technical evaluation of our mangrove-inspired desalination concept revealed multiple biosynthetic and engineering challenges—including post-translational modifications, multi-enzyme requirements, and substrate availability—that made this approach technically infeasible within our project scope.

### Rationale
Prof. AJ Afzal is a plant molecular biologist specializing in protein networks, post-translational modifications, and sub-cellular mechanisms. We consulted him to evaluate our second project concept: engineering bacteria to express mangrove suberin genes for water filtration, biomimicking natural desalination processes.

### Interview Summary
Prof. Afzal confirmed the theoretical feasibility of transferring traits between organisms but identified critical challenges. Post-translational modifications could alter suberin or its transport enzymes in bacteria, requiring complex engineering solutions like PTM-resistant mutants, chaperone overexpression, or alternative host systems. He noted that multiple enzymes likely deposit suberin into cell walls, increasing PTM risk. Essential substrates for suberin biosynthesis might be absent in bacteria, necessitating screening different bacterial species. He warned that bacterial biofilm-based filters would have impractically slow flow rates. He suggested exploring suberin in other organisms or alternatively investigating bacteria that degrade plastics through mutation.

### Integration
Prof. Afzal's comprehensive analysis revealed that our mangrove-inspired approach, while conceptually elegant, presented insurmountable technical barriers for our team's capacity and timeline. The cascading complexity—from uncertain enzyme requirements to substrate availability to post-translational modification management—made this less feasible than initially envisioned. The flow rate limitations further undermined practical viability. This consultation reinforced lessons from our previous pivots: we needed an approach that was scientifically sound but realistically achievable. We ultimately moved away from this desalination concept, continuing our search for a project that balanced innovation with feasibility, eventually returning to focus on CDG therapeutics.

## Fatma Alrebh — Refining Delivery Mechanisms
Card title: Refining Delivery Mechanisms: Technical Guidance from Fatma Alrebh
Date: 20 February 2025

### Summary
Fatma's experience as a two-time iGEM participant and current Asia-Oceania ambassador provided both organizational recommendations and critical technical insights about gene delivery limitations, particularly regarding liposome capacity, transient expression challenges, and immunogenicity concerns that shaped our delivery strategy refinement.

### Rationale
Fatma Alrebh, a two-time iGEM participant, former NYUAD team leader, and current iGEM ambassador for Asia-Oceania, offered both organizational expertise and technical knowledge. We consulted her for team management advice and technical feedback on our evolving gene delivery approach.

### Interview Summary
Fatma recommended registering iGEM as a credited course to help students balance academic workload and securing dedicated laboratory space for the dry lab team. Regarding our gene delivery approach, she noted that liposomes have upper size limits for content delivery, advising us to work with coding sequences only. She warned that without CRISPR genome insertion, effects would be transient. She cautioned that composite gene structures with multiple domains could complicate delivery. Fatma suggested exploring mRNA delivery instead of DNA, noting that mRNA might be more stable in the cytoplasm where it naturally functions. She strongly emphasized researching immunogenicity, as the body can reject introduced genetic material.

### Integration
Fatma's technical concerns about gene delivery—particularly immunogenicity risks and transient expression without genome integration—prompted us to reconsider our delivery mechanism carefully. Her suggestion to explore mRNA rather than DNA delivery opened new possibilities, while her warnings about size limitations and immune rejection informed our design parameters. These insights helped us develop more realistic specifications for our therapeutic approach, prioritizing safety and practical feasibility

## Dr. Andras Gyorgy — From Supplementation to Biomanufacturing
Card title: From Supplementation to Biomanufacturing: Strategic Pivot with Dr. Andras Gyorgy
Date: 25 February 2025

### Summary
Dr. Gyorgy's technical guidance transformed our Man-1-P supplementation concept by providing a rigorous experimental framework for biomanufacturing, addressing both production feasibility and downstream delivery challenges while keeping our undergraduate team's capabilities in focus.

### Rationale
Dr. Andras Gyorgy, Assistant Professor of Electrical Engineering at NYUAD and iGEM PI, specializes in synthetic biology systems and circuit engineering. We consulted him about chassis selection and metabolic engineering strategies for our Man-1-P supplementation approach.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Gyorgy recommended starting with E. coli despite endogenous Man-1-P production, as it's easier to work with. He proposed a dual strategy: upregulating manB (Man-1-P production) while downregulating manC (Man-1-P consumption) using high-copy plasmids with strong inducible promoters rather than genome editing. He suggested using HPLC for yield quantification without extensive purification, noting that carbohydrate impurities from the glycosylation pathway wouldn't harm humans. For delivery applications, he discussed liposome encapsulation and probiotic delivery via engineered E. coli colonizing the small intestine. He mentioned Vibrio natriegens as a sustainable alternative chassis and emphasized demonstrating superior yield and cost-effectiveness.

### Integration
Dr. Gyorgy's guidance provided the experimental foundation for pursuing Man-1-P supplementation seriously. His practical metabolic engineering strategy—upregulating production while downregulating consumption—gave us a concrete, achievable pathway forward. His insights on delivery mechanisms, particularly the probiotic approach, opened possibilities we hadn't fully considered. However, his technical roadmap also revealed the complexity ahead. Combined with subsequent feedback from Dr. Andreotti about supplementation limitations, this consultation helped us recognize we needed to reconsider our fundamental approach entirely.

## Ibrahim Chehade — Understanding Patient Reality

### Summary
Ibrahim's unique dual role as both research PI and caregiver to a family member with PMM2-CDG provided invaluable insights into the lived reality of the disorder—from symptom variability and treatment accessibility to the profound emotional and physical toll on families, grounding our project in genuine patient needs.

### Rationale
Ibrahim Chehade, Instructor of Biology at NYUAD and iGEM PI, has deep personal experience with PMM2-CDG through a family member affected by the condition. His dual perspective as both researcher and caregiver offered crucial insights into clinical realities and family impact.

### Interview Summary
Ibrahim explained PMM2-CDG's spectrum nature—symptoms vary significantly between individuals. His family member experiences ataxia, poor coordination, low muscle tone, and strabismus due to cerebellar impact, alongside delayed milestones and elevated liver enzymes. Current treatments include Epalrestat (a diabetes drug approved in Asia that indirectly improves PMM2 efficiency) and experimental approaches from Glycomine involving liposomal mannose-1-phosphate delivery. Treatment monitoring uses transferrin isoform analysis as a biomarker. Therapy costs approximately 15,000 dirhams monthly, mostly covered by insurance in his case. Ibrahim emphasized the emotional toll—deliberately avoiding reading about progression to focus on present moments, physical strain from mobility assistance, and constant worry about overlooked complications.

### Integration
This conversation profoundly humanized our project. Ibrahim's experience revealed that PMM2-CDG extends far beyond biochemistry—it encompasses daily physical strain, emotional exhaustion, financial burden, and the constant balance between hope and fear. His protective approach to information, choosing present joy over future worry, taught us that families need not just treatments but also accessible monitoring tools and realistic support systems. His mention of therapeutic costs highlighted economic barriers, while his physical strain from mobility assistance inspired our consideration of adaptive equipment. His willingness to discuss such personal challenges, despite the emotional difficulty, demonstrated the courage of CDG families and reinforced our responsibility to pursue solutions that genuinely improve their daily lives, not just laboratory metrics.

## Prof. Wael Rabeh — Metabolic Engineering Realities
Card title: Metabolic Engineering Realities: Technical Consultation with Prof. Wael Rabeh
Date: 4 March 2025

### Summary
Prof. Rabeh's expertise in enzymology and protein chemistry revealed critical metabolic challenges with Man-1-P biomanufacturing—including pathway network complexity, cellular toxicity from phosphorylated sugar accumulation, and membrane transport limitations—that required fundamental reconsideration of our production and purification strategies.

### Rationale
Prof. Wael Rabeh specializes in enzymology, protein structure, and metabolic pathways. We consulted him about biomanufacturing Man-1-P, seeking guidance on in vitro production systems, purification strategies, and computational tools for characterizing PMM2 mutations and potential therapeutic interventions

### Interview Summary
Prof. Rabeh raised critical concerns about stopping metabolic pathways at specific stages given the interconnected pathway networks and reverse enzymes that convert Man-1-P back to Man-6-P. He suggested an in vitro system using mannose, ATP, hexokinase, and PMM2 with column chromatography purification, though noting Man-6-P and Man-1-P separation challenges. He warned that phosphorylated sugars cannot escape cell membranes, limiting accumulation, and at certain concentrations become toxic, causing osmotic pressure that ruptures cells. He recommended mass spectrometry to track hourly Man-1-P production but emphasized the fundamental question of pathway control. For dry lab work, he discussed computational tools for predicting protein-small molecule binding and limitations of PyMOL in capturing protein conformational dynamics.

### Integration
Prof. Rabeh's insights exposed fundamental metabolic barriers we hadn't fully appreciated. The cellular toxicity issue—where phosphorylated sugar accumulation causes osmotic rupture—meant our biomanufacturing approach faced inherent production limits. His emphasis on pathway interconnectedness and reverse reactions highlighted that simply upregulating Man-1-P production (as Dr. Gyorgy suggested) wouldn't guarantee accumulation. The membrane impermeability of phosphorylated sugars complicated both production yield and delivery strategies. These metabolic realities, combined with purification challenges and the core question of pathway control, revealed that biomanufacturing Man-1-P was significantly more complex than initially envisioned. This consultation pushed us toward reconsidering whether supplementation was the right therapeutic paradigm, ultimately contributing to our pivot toward enzyme replacement therapy.

## Dr. Giuseppina Andreotti — Questioning Supplementation
Card title: Questioning Supplementation: Critical Insights from Dr. Giuseppina Andreotti
Date: 11 March 2025

### Summary
Dr. Andreotti's critical evaluation of Man-1-P supplementation revealed fundamental limitations of metabolite replacement approaches, redirecting our focus toward enzyme replacement therapy as a more comprehensive solution for addressing PMM2-CDG at its root cause.

### Rationale
Dr. Giuseppina Andreotti is a chemist specializing in PMM2 protein chemistry and molecular mechanisms. After abandoning PMI inhibition, we consulted her to evaluate our Man-1-P supplementation approach. Her expertise in pharmacological enzyme stabilization and understanding of PMM2 at the molecular level was essential for assessing supplementation viability.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Andreotti expressed skepticism about Man-1-P supplementation's effectiveness, noting that PMM2-CDG's impact likely extends beyond simple metabolite deficiency to include developmental effects. She highlighted major technical challenges: the lack of reliable biomarkers to confirm cellular entry, expensive fluorescent labeling requirements, and inconsistent markers across studies. She suggested alternative activators like Glucose-1,6-bisphosphate or Mannose-1,6-bisphosphate that naturally phosphorylate PMM2 into its active form. Dr. Andreotti advocated strongly for pharmacological approaches using chaperones to protect and stabilize the mutated protein itself, improving its half-life and activity—though effectiveness depends on mutation location relative to the active site.

### Integration
Dr. Andreotti's insights proved crucial in redirecting our approach from supplementation to enzyme replacement therapy. Her skepticism about Man-1-P being comprehensive—noting that effects might stem from embryonic development rather than just metabolite absence—challenged our assumption that supplementation alone could address PMM2-CDG. Her emphasis on pharmacological approaches to restore PMM2 activity itself resonated deeply: why supplement a missing product when you could restore the factory producing it? This represented a paradigm shift from treating symptoms to addressing root causes. We inclined toward enzyme replacement therapy, recognizing that restoring functional PMM2 would provide the most complete therapeutic solution.

Dr. Garapati's dual perspective as a physician-scientist provided crucial insights into the clinical heterogeneity of PMM2-CDG, the limitations of current biomarkers like transferrin, and the importance of understanding molecular consequences at the cellular level, while emphasizing how simple interventions like monosaccharide supplementation can meaningfully improve patient symptoms.

## Loganathan Palanikumar — Optimizing Therapeutic Delivery
Card title: Optimizing Therapeutic Delivery: Engineering Insights from Loganathan Palanikumar
Date: 14 March 2025

### Summary
Loganathan's expertise in drug delivery systems provided practical protocols for characterizing and optimizing Man-1-P delivery through PEGylated liposomes and PLGA nanoparticles, while also highlighting critical immunogenicity concerns with bacterial extracellular vesicles that influenced our delivery mechanism selection.

### Rationale
Loganathan Palanikumar, a senior research scientist at NYUAD specializing in nanoparticle drug delivery systems, provided technical guidance on delivery mechanisms for our Man-1-P supplementation approach, including liposome formulation, characterization protocols, and comparative analysis of delivery platforms.

### Interview Summary
Loganathan recommended comparing PEGylated liposomes and PLGA nanoparticles as untargeted delivery systems for Man-1-P. He outlined comprehensive characterization protocols: electron microscopy for morphology, dynamic light scattering for size determination, and zeta potential measurements for surface charge assessment (noting Man-1-P's negative charge from phosphate groups should provide stability). For cargo quantification, he suggested HPLC and LC/MS analysis, emphasizing that method development would take weeks but data analysis was most crucial. He cautioned against bacterial extracellular vesicles due to immunogenicity risks from bacterial proteins and materials that are difficult to characterize, recommending that any bacterial delivery system use human microbiome-resident organisms like E. coli.

### Integration
Loganathan's practical roadmap gave us concrete experimental protocols for delivery system development and characterization. His detailed characterization methods—from electron microscopy to zeta potential measurements—provided the framework for rigorous delivery optimization. However, his strong caution about bacterial extracellular vesicle immunogenicity was sobering, highlighting safety concerns we hadn't fully considered. The realization that characterizing immunogenic materials in bEVs would be extremely difficult added another layer of complexity. Combined with metabolic production challenges from Prof. Rabeh and supplementation skepticism from Dr. Andreotti, this consultation further reinforced that our Man-1-P supplementation approach, while theoretically sound, faced mounting practical obstacles that demanded reconsideration of our fundamental therapeutic strategy

## Dr. Hicham Alaoui of Glycomine — Industry Insights from the Frontlines
Card title: Industry Insights from the Frontlines: Consultation with Dr. Hicham Alaoui of Glycomine
Date: 20 March 2025

### Summary
Dr. Alaoui's perspective as Chief Scientific Officer at Glycomine—the only company actively developing PMM2-CDG therapeutics in clinical trials—provided invaluable validation of the Man-1-P supplementation approach while revealing the immense regulatory, production, and accessibility challenges inherent in rare disease therapeutic development that shaped our understanding of translational barriers.

### Rationale
Dr. Hicham Alaoui is Chief Scientific Officer at Glycomine, the leading company developing Man-1-P supplementation therapy (GLM101) for PMM2-CDG currently in clinical trials. His extensive pharmaceutical industry experience and direct involvement in translating CDG research into viable therapeutics made him uniquely positioned to evaluate our approach and provide insights into market realities.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Alaoui explained Glycomine's strategic decision to deliver Man-1-P rather than GDP-mannose, choosing the simpler molecule closest to the enzymatic defect to minimize biological complications. While GLM101 primarily localizes to the liver due to its high glycosylation rate, in vivo testing showed distribution to other organs as well. He confirmed that Man-1-P instability in blood stems from enzymatic hydrolysis by circulating enzymes, resulting in minute-scale half-lives before liver or kidney clearance. Regarding production, Glycomine manufactures chemical-grade Man-1-P themselves due to high commercial costs driven by chemical synthesis requirements and limited market dynamics. On biomanufacturing alternatives, he suggested exploring published in vitro assays using coupled reactions and investigating other enzymes capable of producing Man-1-P, though he noted directed enzyme evolution—while Nobel Prize-winning—exceeded iGEM scope. He emphasized purification challenges would be greater from bacterial systems than reaction vessels. For clinical translation, he described navigating complex regulatory landscapes, requiring safety demonstration in healthy individuals during Phase I trials before patient studies. Glycomine's trials currently span the US, EU, and UK, with interest in expanding to the Middle East if community need exists.

### Integration
This consultation provided a crucial industry perspective that both validated and challenged our approach. Dr. Alaoui's explanation of why Glycomine chose Man-1-P over GDP-mannose—prioritizing molecular simplicity—countered some of Dr. Andreotti's skepticism about supplementation efficacy. However, his confirmation of Man-1-P's blood instability and rapid enzymatic degradation highlighted a fundamental challenge: even if we successfully biomanufactured Man-1-P, delivering it effectively to cells remained problematic. His mention of chemical production costs and suggestion to explore in vitro enzymatic synthesis validated our biomanufacturing direction as potentially valuable for reducing therapeutic costs. Most significantly, his insights into regulatory complexity, clinical trial requirements, and the need to demonstrate safety before efficacy revealed the enormous translational gap between laboratory proof-of-concept and accessible patient therapies. Understanding that Glycomine—with substantial resources and pharmaceutical expertise—was still navigating these challenges helped us recognize our role: we could contribute to the foundational science and potentially reduce production costs, but comprehensive therapeutic development required sustained, well-funded efforts beyond our scope. This realistic assessment helped refocus our project on achievable contributions while maintaining awareness of the broader therapeutic landscape.

## Dr. Edgar Gonzalez-Kozlova — Exploring EV Delivery Mechanisms
Card title: Exploring EV Delivery Mechanisms: Technical Discussion with Dr. Edgar Gonzalez-Kozlova
Date: 20 March 2025

### Summary
Dr. Gonzalez-Kozlova's computational biology expertise and hands-on experience with extracellular vesicle isolation provided practical protocols for EV-based delivery, while his emphasis on proof-of-concept feasibility and recommendation of size exclusion chromatography offered an achievable experimental pathway despite concerns about yield limitations and biosafety considerations.

### Rationale
Dr. Edgar Gonzalez-Kozlova is a computational biologist specializing in cellular signaling and extracellular vesicle research. We consulted him about using EVs—particularly bacterial EVs—as delivery vehicles for either Man-1-P or PMM2 mRNA, seeking guidance on isolation protocols, cargo loading mechanisms, and experimental feasibility.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Gonzalez-Kozlova, while lacking direct bacterial EV experience, provided valuable insights from his work with mammalian cell-derived EVs. He typically uses immortalized cancer cell lines like prostate cancer line 22RV1, which produces ten-fold more EVs than normal cells, and works primarily with cDNAs rather than mRNAs. He reassured us that low EV yields shouldn't concern an iGEM proof-of-concept project. For our purposes, he believed bacteria would be the easiest organism choice since virtually all cells secrete EVs. He strongly recommended size exclusion chromatography for EV isolation over ultracentrifugation—it's faster, more reproducible, gentler, and relatively inexpensive. The process yields broad EV peaks due to size heterogeneity, followed by cell-free DNA and protein fractions. EV generation takes approximately one week. He noted that 1mL of concentrated EV solution typically yields 500μL fractions through the chromatography process.

### Integration
Dr. Gonzalez-Kozlova's practical protocols made EV-based delivery seem more experimentally accessible than we initially thought. His recommendation of size exclusion chromatography over ultracentrifugation removed a significant technical barrier. This consultation added to our growing realization that simpler, better-characterized delivery mechanisms might be more appropriate for our timeline and capabilities.

## Dr. Iván Duncker and CDG CARE — Bridging Continents and Communities

Date: 21 March 2025

### Summary
Dr. Duncker's unique perspective as a researcher working in an underserved region revealed systemic barriers in rare disease diagnosis and treatment that extend far beyond scientific challenges—including lack of technological infrastructure, absent genomic databases, physician education gaps, and the dual odysseys of diagnosis and treatment that CDG families face, particularly in developing regions, ultimately leading to our sustained collaboration with CDG CARE.

Dr. Iván Duncker is Scientific Advisor and Director of Research at CDG CARE, a family-founded organization supporting CDG patients globally. Based in Mexico, his work focuses on establishing diagnostic protocols and raising awareness in underserved regions. We consulted him to understand global CDG challenges, particularly in areas lacking resources comparable to the US and Europe.

### Interview Summary
Dr. Duncker's journey into CDG research began during his PhD in France studying glycoscience, where he helped describe a new CDG type. Returning to Mexico in 2006, he found zero documented CDG cases despite the country's population. His diagnostic approach involved building alliances with neurologists, establishing transferrin isoform screening protocols, and collaborating with geneticists for genome sequencing—though regions lacking genomic databases struggle interpreting unknown variants. He described the dual burden: a diagnostic odyssey averaging ten years, followed by a therapeutic odyssey where fewer than five percent of rare diseases have specific treatments. Underdeveloped regions lag behind therapeutically despite diagnostic progress. He never charges patients and sends samples to Mayo Clinic despite costs. His awareness strategies include face-to-face hospital visits, conference presentations, case report publications, residency program teaching, undergraduate rare disease courses, and Latin American glycobiology congresses. At CDG CARE, funded by the Chan-Zuckerberg Initiative, he advises research efforts, creating unique dynamics where scientists, families, and clinicians interact, strengthening global collaboration.

### Integration
Dr. Duncker's consultation fundamentally expanded our understanding beyond molecular biology into global health equity. His revelation that Mexico had zero documented CDG cases in 2006—despite surely having patients—illustrated how rare diseases become invisible without diagnostic infrastructure and physician awareness.This directly inspired our Physician education initiatives if doctors don't learn about CDG in medical school, diagnosis becomes impossible. His concept of dual odysseys (diagnostic and therapeutic) revealed that developing treatments addresses only half the problem. His awareness-raising strategies—particularly face-to-face hospital outreach and undergraduate education—provided models we adapted. Most significantly, his suggestion to develop a diagnostic referral laboratory network in the MENA region became cornerstone to our work. We compiled a physician directory connecting CDG-knowledgeable doctors with regional patients, directly addressing the diagnostic odyssey. Dr. Duncker's continued involvement as expert speaker at our physician conference demonstrated CDG CARE's commitment to empowering emerging researchers globally, reminding us that rare disease work requires building sustainable infrastructure, educational programs, and collaborative networks beyond individual therapeutic development.

His recommendation of E. coli strains with modified LPS directly addressed our primary biosafety concern regarding immunogenicity. The detailed purification protocols—particularly density gradient centrifugation for high purity—gave us rigorous methods to characterize our bEV cargo and ensure we could distinguish vesicle-encapsulated content from extracellular contamination. His shared protocols and papers on hypervesiculating strains provided the starting point for selecting appropriate bacterial strains. This guidance transformed bEV delivery from a theoretical possibility into a concrete experimental pathway with established methodologies we could adapt for PMM2 mRNA delivery.

Date: 10 April 2025

### Summary
Dr. Steet's expertise in zebrafish and yeast models for CDG research provided technical guidance on delivery targeting, metabolic flux concerns with PMM2 overexpression, and the importance of accompanying metabolite studies to assess treatment efficacy beyond simple glycosylation restoration.

### Rationale
Dr. Richard Steet investigates molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying CDG and lysosomal diseases using zebrafish and cell-based models. His work on glycan-related pathologies and therapeutic approaches made him valuable for evaluating our delivery strategy and discussing model organism considerations.

Dr. Steet entered CDG research through his fascination with how sugar modifications alter proteins and their manifestation in severe patient phenotypes. Regarding our project, he raised concerns about delivery targeting: families' primary concern—central nervous system effects—requires neural tissue delivery, not just liver targeting. He cautioned that overexpressing PMM2 beyond certain thresholds can shift metabolic flux unintentionally, recommending inclusion of mRNA for other enzymes to maintain balance and emphasizing metabolite studies alongside treatment. For efficacy measurement, he suggested various precursors, activity assays, or NADH production monitoring. He mentioned PMM2-deficient yeast strains can be evolved to find viable variants.

### Integration
Dr. Steet's consultation highlighted delivery and metabolic considerations we had not fully addressed.
