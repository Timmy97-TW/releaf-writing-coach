Introduction
Research inherently involves risk, and its minimization is a top priority for both iGEM and our team. We are committed to safeguarding our team members during the project, while also ensuring the safety of the environment and the broader population in future applications of our work. Therefore, we not only followed the safety regulations of iGEM as well as legislative requirements, but also proactively reached out to experts in this field to assess risks, challenges and mitigation strategies related to our topic.
Safety in the lab
In our project Tarakate, we limited our research to only work with German Biosafety Level 1 (S1) approved organisms, such as Escherichia coli, Agrobacterium rhizogenes and Sinorhizobium meliloti. All work was conducted in approved S1 laboratories. Additionally, all team members received a mandatory safety briefing, before they were allowed access to the laboratory space. All work was carried out following the legal S1 regulations for Germany.
All parts used or designed during this project are either listed on the iGEM whitelist or have undergone the necessary approval processes through proper applications. We recognize the importance of mitigating risks associated with laboratory work, particularly the spread of antibiotic resistances. Therefore, we have ensured that antibiotics were employed responsibly and strictly within the guidelines for safe usage.
While no research is without risks, we have taken extensive measures to minimize any potential risks posed by our genetic parts and constructs to both human health and the environment. These efforts include careful consideration of biosafety and biosecurity practices throughout the project. Additionally, our work fully complies with all safety regulations and protocols set forth by the iGEM competition.
To ensure our research is not only safe but also secure, we reached out to Dr. Svenja Vinke, a member of the iGEM Safety & Security Committee, to gain further insights into the topic. She noted that biosecurity concerns are often overlooked in academia. When asked how to address this issue, she responded with the following:
> The only way, how this system can actually change, [is] by having more and more people aware that biosecurity risks exist.
- Dr. Svenja Vinke
Inspired by her call to action, we committed ourselves to raising awareness within our team and took concrete steps to address biosecurity risks. By leveraging tools provided by the Biosecurity Bureau in the Netherlands, we enhanced our awareness of potential dual-use concerns. More importantly, we adapted our practices to mitigate any identified risks. A critical component of our strategy involved implementing measures to restrict access to both our laboratory space and in silico data for non-team members.
Navigating Future Safety Considerations in Agriculture
One of the major concerns pertaining to the cultivation of genetically modified (GM) crops is the possibility of unforeseen consequences in case they escape the intended field and are introduced into a wild ecosystem. Despite the fact that no biological hazard has ever been attributed to GM organism (GMO) spread so far, the fact remains that preventing such risk is a top priority for scientists and lawmakers worldwide (1). Because of that, the iGEM community requires teams to acknowledge all possible biosafety and biosecurity issues of their projects, and strongly encourages them to develop solutions for such issues in the lab.
One of the main routes for the spread of GM traits is through cross-pollination with wild varieties, leading to the escape of transgenes into the native gene-pool. Alternatively, cross-breeding between cultivated fields via pollen may lead to contamination of non-GMO food supplies with GMO material. This has already been documented and resulted in million dollar law-suits over the past two and a half decades (2). In order to prevent this, farmers are encouraged to create buffer zones between GM and conventional crops. These zones can be strips of empty land, physical barriers such as trees, or plots planted with non-GM crops that are sold together with the GM harvest. While their effectiveness is dependent on the type of buffer zone and the type of crop, multiple studies have shown that separating the two fields by 10 to 20 m can already drastically decrease the chance of cross-pollination (3).
Because there is disagreement on what the ideal strategy for biocontainment is, countries vary in the requirements for buffer zone size. In the US, there is no federal law requiring buffer zones around GM fields, although some regulations have been passed at State levels. Mostly, the USDA (United States Department of Agriculture) issues guidelines that encourage the implementation of buffer zones, while the exact implementation (type and width) remains for the farmer to decide.
In the European Union, on the other hand, regulation on the measures to prevent GM escape are much more stringent. Only a select few GM crops have been approved for use, and a buffer zone of 20 m is required by the EFSA (European Food Safety Authority). Additionally, countries inside the EU have implemented their own regulations. For instance, Germany has enacted a blanket-ban on GM crops in 2009, and prior to that it required a buffer distance of 150 m.
Ensuring Safety in Dandelion-Driven Agriculture
While the more common dandelion variety primarily reproduces through self-fertilization and exhibits a very low pollination rate, the Kazakh variety (Taraxacum kok-saghyz) relies on pollination, making it particularly relevant to our research. The likelihood of cross-pollination significantly decreases with increasing distance between plants, underscoring the importance of managing plant populations effectively. To mitigate potential risks associated with cross-pollination, implementing legislation that enforces the use of buffer zones and other physical barriers is a critical first step (4).
In the case of dandelions, seed architecture poses an extra challenge. The parachute-like fluff (pappus) allows dandelion seeds to disperse over large distances. And although most seeds (99.5%) land within a 10 m radius, conducive weather conditions with strong updraft wind may increase this distance to more than 1 kilometer (5). This not only creates the risk of GMO spread to native ecosystems and other cultivated fields, but also to nearby pasture where it can become feedstock for cattle (6).
Since it is implausible to completely eliminate any spread of GM crops, we reached out to an expert on biosecurity of dandelion, Katja Thiele.
> I am not of the opinion that dandelion, in any form, poses a [ecological] risk.
- Katja Thiele
Thiele has studied several species of dandelions and their hybridization. She informed us that the Russian dandelion is unable to outcompete local weeds, and that in general cultivated crops are not well adapted to the wild environment. This makes it unlikely for them to grow outside a controlled habitat, further underlying the low risk for dandelion. She concluded by stating that the introduction of a novel Taraxacum species is highly unlikely to significantly impact the environment, due to the German ecosystem being already well adapted to a vast variety of different Taraxacum species (7).
In conclusion, our commitment to biosafety and biosecurity throughout this project has been crucial in guiding our research practices and decision-making processes. By meticulously following established safety regulations, engaging with experts, and implementing rigorous risk mitigation strategies, we strived to ensure the responsible use of genetically modified dandelion. We acknowledge that research inherently involves risks and our proactive approach aims to minimize potential impacts on human health and the environment. We recognize the importance of ongoing vigilance and adaptability as we navigate the complexities of genetic engineering, and we are dedicated to fostering a culture of safety that prioritizes the well-being of our team, the ecosystem, and society as a whole.
