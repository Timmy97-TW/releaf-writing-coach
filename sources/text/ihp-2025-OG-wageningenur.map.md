## Dr. Jacob P. Rohn — CEO of SeedForward
Map group: Industry

> “We have partners who would be willing to invest in something like that, it's a really interesting approach (the BT protein for bioinsecticide/clickbeetle use)… we have nearly no insecticides left that are really working on the seed, and if you have a natural solution that would work, we could definitely organise someone who would finance it, because that is an important issue.”

Why we reached out: We identified SeedForward as having a mission similar to ours, as they offer raw materials to formulate customised solutions for different crops. We realised they provide technical support and market expertise in the research, development, and marketing of seed products.

Main takeaways:

1. They mainly focus on the formulation of seed coatings and obtain their matrix solutions from suppliers.
2. They transitioned from a bioplastic-based solution to a starch-based solution.
3. The biggest impact of plastics reduction can be made in coated artificial fertilisers.
4. Minimal requirements for seed coatings are: a stable formulation for at least 12 months (preferably 24 months) and a homogeneous solution.
5. Some microplastic-free polymers are available, but they still face quality and stability challenges.
6. Bringing new agrochemicals or biologicals to market takes a long time and is very expensive due to extensive safety and legal requirements set by lawmakers.
7. Field trials are essential for validating market claims and building consumer trust; this should be a significant part of the initial budget.
8. We discussed potential arrangements for working with corn seeds to create a coating against wireworm.
9. We also discussed conducting field trials with support from SeedForward.

Reflection & integration: We found that our BC coatings with a bioinsecticide protein, which have been shown to reduce mealworm feeding, would be of high interest to SeedForward’s customers. They welcomed us to their facility in Osnabrück to conduct larger-scale coating trials. Their feedback helped us develop a realistic timeline for product development.

## Maroushka Blahetek — CEO and co-founder of Carapace Biopolymers
Map group: Industry

Why we reached out: Similarly to our values, Carapace Biopolymers is a start-up that has made it its mission to help agricultural companies and farmers transition away from polluting petrochemicals and microplastic-polluting materials, and to create plant resilience through their bio-coating solutions.

Main takeaways:

1. Three seasons of field trials are needed.
2. The market is rigid and reputation is important, since farmers do not want to risk their yields.
3. Either the price or the performance needs to outperform conventional coatings.
4. Sales could be made to intermediate customers like seed companies instead of end users.
5. The market is niche, and technical information about seed coatings is often private.
6. We should seek opportunities to replace agrochemicals with biologicals.
7. The colour of the seed coating is very important; it signals consistency in the quality of seeds for farmers.
8. Main desired characteristics for seed coatings: long storage, good germination, and prevention of dust formation during the coating process.
9. The final form of the coating can be powder or liquid.
10. Seeds, locations, and farmers each have their own requirements. Different requirements can be fulfilled by the coating itself or by the active ingredients added to the coating.
11. One platform material for all seed-coating applications is the “holy grail” many are looking for.

Reflection & integration:

1. We began brainstorming how to make our biodegradable seed coatings compatible with different applications so they can serve as a platform that replaces agrochemicals with biologicals.
2. We coloured our coated seeds with natural dyes and with chromophore proteins.
3. We started looking for opportunities to help us establish a reputation in the market and among our customers.

## Amalia Kafka — Technical Manager of Sustainable Seed Technologies at Euroseeds
Map group: Industry

Why we reached out: As a seed lobbying group, they represent the interests of active stakeholders in the research, breeding, production, and marketing of seeds and seed technologies for various plant species.

Main takeaways: Availability of biodegradable active compounds within seed coats is a problem. However, the development of seed coating materials without microplastics has been in progress for a while. More time is needed for these novel products to be implemented.

Reflection & integration: There have already been efforts from the market to transition into biodegradable coatings; however, the deadline for the regulations is approaching, and time is limited. This made us realise that collaboration with businesses that have experience, equipment and infrastructure will be vital in meeting the deadline. Additionally, we realised that only having a biodegradable material is not novel, and our value proposition should highlight the ease of incorporating desired active compounds into the coating.

## Jeroen van Rotterdam — Entrepreneur and CEO of Foamlab
Map group: Industry

> “The coating is a major pitfall. Agriculture uses an awful lot of plastic, so something else definitely needs to be found. But it's not just in the seeds. Agriculture is one of the top 10 plastic-polluting industries, so it's getting a lot of attention. That's why there's definitely potential.”

Why we reached out: At Foamlab, they produce bionanocellulose foams with tunable properties. They focus on replacing materials with sustainable alternatives. Foamlab is a startup transitioning from lab to market. They use the same material as we do. We wanted to discover the BC market and exchange opinions about our production process.

Main takeaways:

1. BC is already produced on a large scale for nata de coco. The second-largest BC production is for wound coatings.
2. Foamlab makes foams with different strength and elasticity by changing both the fermentation (GMO) and the downstream processing. They also add other compostable materials to change the properties.
3. 100,000 L production is needed when they are upscaling.
4. Plant cellulose has a harsh production process, including harsh chemicals. BC is much more environmentally friendly and grows faster than wood cellulose. Additionally, BC is very pure and well absorbed by the human body.
5. The market for bioplastics grows faster than the market for petroplastics, so bioplastics are interesting to invest in. This is more impactful than regulations alone.
6. BC can be sterilised by cooking. This doesn not change the structure if the structure is pure.
7. BC production can be done efficiently enough for products such as packaging, if it is produced at a large scale.
8. The whole production of oil-based polymers is more difficult than BC.
9. The crystal structure is very directional. It depends on whether you grow the material statically or in a bioreactor.
10. The optimal downstream processing method can highly depend on the structure of the BC. Ideally, you find a downstream method that works for all structures.
11. A pellicle is difficult to coat a seed with. If you degrade and rebuild the pellicle in downstream processing, you change the characteristics.
12. BC is very sticky, which makes it complicated to use as a coating.

Reflection & integration: We had an early warning that the stickiness of BC might create a caveat in coating experiments—which it did—and we sought help from Dr. Zohaib Hussain , who has expertise in physical chemistry and BC.

## Şevval Kocaman — Bioengineer and lead of R&D at SUMATRIX Biotech
Map group: Industry

Why we reached out: SUMATRIX Biotech is an innovative biopolymer company that, through academic research, develops methods for optimising the bacterial cellulose (BC) production process. They focus on sustainable agriculture with vertical and hydroponic farming, as well as the cosmetics industry.

Main takeaways:

1. For feedstock, they use winery waste (grape-juice byproducts), which is hydrolysed and centrifuged to obtain a sugar-rich mixture.
2. Composition varies and affects the mechanical structure, but yield is their primary concern rather than consistency of structure.
3. They tune BC properties by adjusting process parameters (e.g., incubation time) or substrate composition depending on the application.
4. On a larger scale, clean rooms are a must to avoid contamination. Sterilisation with UV light is utilised, but there is risk since Komagataeibacter is UV-sensitive.
5. Their core focus is production of BC; the application of choice is driven by market demand.
6. Native cellulose colour is not desirable for applications.
7. Natural colouration is achieved by boiling in pigmented solutions (e.g., coffee for a brown, leather-like colour).

Reflection & integration: Inspired by their colouring method for BC leathers, we experimented with dyeing BC using natural colourings. As brighter colours are preferred in the seed sector, we tried dried strawberry powder, red beet powder, and spirulina powder. We also accounted for the clean rooms required for larger-scale production in our cost analysis.

## Bio Base Europe — A pilot facility for process development, scale-up and custom manufacturing of innovative bio-based products and processes.
Map group: Industry

Why we visited: We wanted to learn about how the scale-up process works and what could be done in the R&D stage to avoid having to change an aspect of the production project because it is not in accordance with scale-up considerations. Moreover, we would like to use facilities developed to help in scaling up instead of allocating more resources for this.

Main takeaways:

Karel de Winter, head of Technology Development at Bio Base Europe Pilot Plant, gave us a tour of the facility. We got a chance to see equipment and setups that are used to test the scaling up of laboratory designs. In addition, we discussed the piloting of our process. Scaling up needs a lot of equipment and a lot of testing. To prevent large investments in capital expenditures that will be useless in later scaling-up phases, it is better to use a pilot plant during the piloting phase.

We learned that there is no infrastructure for the process we initially envisioned; stacking horizontal bioreactors for scaling up is very uncommon. If we aim to do a pilot for scaling up this way, we would have to build the pilot plant ourselves. This would require large investments of around 5 million and a large risk. It is also possible to produce bacterial cellulose with a continuous stirred tank reactor, as seven processes for this have already been scaled up at Bio Base Europe. This piloting would be much more cost-efficient.

Another topic we discussed is the sterilisation of our product. Karel de Winter pointed out the costs of chemical sterilisation of our product. He suggested that we look at methods they use to remove DNA from corn and fungi, as these food products have some of their DNA removed by heat and washing before consumption.

Finally, we discussed alternative feedstocks. The use of alternative feedstocks can decrease production costs, but it is important to take a few things into consideration when choosing a feedstock. The feedstock has to be continuously available in larger amounts than you need. The complexity of the waste stream also has a large influence on the costs. Purification steps are very costly, and condensing very diluted waste streams also brings complications.

Reflection & integration: Piloting is an important phase in business development, between research and development and scaling up. This is a risky part for many start-ups, also referred to as the valley of death. The conversations we had with Karel de Winter helped us in the development of our entrepreneurial plan, so that we can pass this valley of death and reach the scale-up phase. We decided to design our production in stirred tank reactors. In addition, Karel de Winter helped us identify the calculations we have to do to assess the economic viability of our product. We also considered the remarks on alternative feedstocks, which led us to select a local waste stream from Stadsbrouwerij Rad van Wageningen , as this waste stream is highly condensed. It only has to be diluted, and not many purification steps are needed.

## Anonymous arable farmer — A Dutch arable farmer managing around 45 hectares.
Map group: Farmers

Why we reached out: To gain insights into practical experiences with seeds, coatings, and agricultural innovations. We wanted to understand how a farmer deals with biotic and abiotic stressors, climate change, and sustainability challenges, as well as how he makes decisions about seed (coating) choice, suppliers, and technology adoption.

Main takeaways:

1. He grows potatoes, sugar beets, wheat, and maize.
2. The farmer experiences an increase in extreme weather, including heavy rain, flooding, or prolonged dry periods. Crops like potatoes can only survive in a flooded area for 24 hours before rotting starts. Currently, he digs trenches for the water. He finds the unpredictability frustrating.
3. He uses pelleted (a type of coating) seeds for sugar beet. This is because the seeds are uneven in shape, which makes sowing difficult. By pelleting, they are easier to sow and dose on the field. This saves time, and the coatings attract moisture to increase germination in dry periods.
4. He finds the bans on certain products too abrupt; sometimes, products are banned before a good alternative is on the market.
5. He is aware of pesticide coatings, which he used for sugar beet and wheat. Bans on neonicotinoids now force him to spray against pests instead of using coatings. This costs more time, and spraying is worse in performance, according to him.
6. He was not aware of the presence of microplastics in coatings, as he always used the same coating.
7. He is looking for innovation, as long as it is financially possible. For example, he is open to using AI to help with sorting and has tested biostimulants in the field. However, the biostimulants did not seem effective.

Reflection & integration: The unpredictability in weather is frustrating to farmers, and we should come up with a solution that would, for example, prevent rotting when flooding occurs—such as tuning the water-holding capacity or hydrophobicity of the coatings.

There is a gap between the regulatory requirements and the struggles of farmers. We should consider this when complying with new policies, avoid neglecting the impacts on farmers, and develop mitigation strategies in collaboration with other sector stakeholders.

## Egge Jan Hommes — Arable farmer growing sugar beet, onion, different wheats, potatoes and works together with an ecological farmer
Map group: Farmers

Why we reached out: Even though our business model is B2B, interviewing farmers was essential because they are the end-users most directly affected by the problems we are designing our product to solve. We value including the voice of the people whose livelihoods depend on resilient seed, and care about customising our solutions according to the most detrimental conditions for their crops.

Main takeaways:
About his struggles with crops and characteristics of seed coatings:

1. Profiting from potatoes is a challenge. They experienced low yields due to fungal infections for a while. This year, the yield was better since it was drier, but then the market overflowed, and their prices were very low.
2. The onion and sugar beets he uses are coated with fungicides and insecticides that support even germination and growth. The fungicide aspect is a part of the seed coating material; the insecticides are an optional add-on by his seed supplier. Insects eat the beet leaves first, which kills the plant.
3. He plants his own cereals, which are not coated. You can get your own seeds coated, but he did not think this was worth investing in since they grow well. When he buys other cereal seeds, it is to introduce a new species, and these are coated.
4. He finds seed coatings very effective (low dosage of active compound is sufficient) and impactful. For sowing, only his pelleted beet seeds make a difference.
5. He does not know or care that microplastics are in seed coatings, as they are in everything; he says it would not affect his choice.
6. He cares about the crop breed that is patented by the supplier, not which company he buys his seeds from. He changes to a new breed sometimes. He believes all large seed suppliers in the Netherlands are reputable enough.

About his opinions on policies:

1. As the government bans more pesticides, the usable ones get scarcer, and the crops develop resistance against chemical groups. The farmer suffers losses, and the food supply is at risk for everyone.
2. He thinks that the bans are sometimes made without considering the negative effects they might inflict.

About his opinions on our BCoated coatings:

1. He thinks that yield performance is key, even if it is more environmental; if the yields are less, it is not more sustainable or affordable in the long term.
2. He believes our insecticide can work for a range of insects.

Reflection & integration: Higher performance in yield is the most important factor for him. We have considered this in our competitor analysis.

He thinks seed coatings are impactful and especially effective as a way of delivering active compounds. We believe our seed coatings can reduce the amount of fertilisers and pesticides used.

A main stressor for sugar beets is insects, while a main stressor for potatoes is fungal infections in wet seasons. We should think about enzymatic modifications to our BC for fungicidal properties, and implement insecticides on the matrix.

## Dr. Shanmugam Thiyagajaran — Expert in polymer chemistry at Sustainable Chemistry and Technology Group, Wageningen University & Research
Map group: Academia

Why we reached out: We were considering several biodegradable and biobased polymers to design our seed coatings and wanted to learn about the pros and cons of several options.

Main takeaways: We discussed the properties of PHA and BC and found out that BC contains reactive hydroxyl groups.

Reflection & integration: We may be able to exploit the reactive hydroxyl groups in BC to enzymatically modify the matrix. This would be a greener method than adding chemical modifications to alter the water-holding capacity and hydrophobicity of the seed coating. These properties help control the germination time of the seed. We integrated this into our wet lab design and developed a project to test the effect of different enzymatic modifications.

## Dr. ir. ing. Dennis Oonincx — Expert in insects, nutrition and circular economy at Animal Nutrition Group, Wageningen University & Research
Map group: Academia

Why we reached out: When starting out with the project, we initially wanted to validate the effectiveness of our protein delivery system by displaying its effect on a common pest insect, namely Helicoverpa armigera. We reached out to Dr. ir. ing. Dennis Oonincx from the Animal Nutrition group, who specialises in using insects — including mealworms — as food and feed.

Main takeaways: Together with Dennis, we developed a laboratory protocol to test the effect of the Cry3Aa protein on mealworms. Although mealworms are not common seed pests, they are a great model organism for insect systems because they are easy to rear and handle, and because of their voracious appetite. Dennis, together with his thesis student Myrthe Duijsens, also gave us a “mealworm tutorial”, showing us how to handle insects and ensuring a smooth course during our experiments.

Reflection & integration: Speaking with Dennis revealed a lot of incorrect assumptions about working with insects. Due to his advice, we were able to avoid common mistakes that would easily be made by researchers inexperienced in working with insects.

## Dr. Amritpal Singh — PhD (Imperial College London) working with biomaterials and soil microbiomes
Map group: Academia

Why we reached out: We were referred to him to further discuss why bacterial cellulose (BC) could be an advantageous material for implementing in the soil. We discussed what strain we should use and the type of bioreactor that would yield the most BC.

Main takeaways:

1. Komagataeibacter sucrofermentans, a subspecies of K. xylinus, was recommended to us as it achieves higher yields due to its more complete energy metabolism.
2. A stacked tray bioreactor for producing BC is the most efficient when producing sheets; this way, you can scale up horizontally instead of vertically.

Reflection & integration: We decided to work with K. sucrofermentans, and we started thinking about our bioreactor design for scale-up and how to prevent contamination. We considered this tray design for our entrepreneurship, but after conversations with Bio Base Europe , we decided to use a different production strategy after all.

## Prof. dr. ir. Jan van den Ende — Professor of Management of Technology and Innovation at Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University Rotterdam. Specialised in horticulture and food.
Map group: Academia

Why we reached out: One of our team members had a connection to him, and we wanted to take advantage of his expertise in evaluating innovation potential, scalability, and strategic implementation for novel technologies. Moreover, we predicted that he might have contacts within the seed sector.

Main takeaways:

1. We should look at the three parts of the supply chain (network, processes, flows) and the business model of all parties. We should consider why they would want to use our product.

They have different knowledge and therefore different requirements for the product. In addition, Prof. dr. ir. Jan van den Ende connected us to Maroushka Blahetek from Carapace Biopolymers.

## Dr. Desalegn W. Etalo — Assistant Professor in Translational Phytopathology at Phytopathology group, Wageningen University & Research. He strives to develop biodiversity-centred resilient agricultural or ecological intervention strategies to counter the current decline in agroecosystems.
Map group: Academia

Why we reached out: We were considering incorporating microbes as a carrier of nutrients, antimicrobial or antifungal compounds. He is knowledgeable about soil microbiomes.

Main takeaways:

1. Microbes can be rejected and not incorporated into the native community.
2. Introduced microbes can also dominate and overrun the rhizosphere—potentially creating new problems while solving others.
3. “Microbial fishing” could be an interesting approach: adding precursor molecules that attract specific microbes in the soil.
4. The precursor can be selected according to which microbes you want to attract.

Reflection & integration: We need to rethink including microbes and design modular seed coatings for different applications. For precursor molecules to “fish” with, we could reach out to Prof. dr. Jonne Rodenburg, who is researching a VOC precursor molecule as a strategy against a parasite.

## Dr. ir. Bert Smit — Son of a farmer, Researcher \& account manager, Northern Netherlands at Wageningen University & Research Social & Economic Research Group (formerly Agr. Econ. Res. Inst. Leiden)
Map group: Academia

> “The start of a crop is very important, so a seed coating helps enormously to give the crop a good start, allowing you to quickly get a strong plant and crop.” “In my view, it is useful to use seed coatings, because that way you can ensure a good start and prevent a whole range of problems later on.”

Why we reached out: As an expert in European agricultural policy and sustainable arable farming who views his mission to be connecting the agricultural sector with policymakers, we thought he would be a perceptive stakeholder who is widely aware of the different problems and demands of various actors in the sector.

Main takeaways:

1. Responsibility for sustainability in agriculture lies with consumers, farmers, and the government.
2. The EU offers eco-premiums for organic farmers to stimulate the transition to more sustainable farming.
3. Farmers are willing to test new products; you need to find pioneering farmers as early adopters.
4. The initial growth and establishment of a crop is very important. This is even more important for organic farmers. Seed coatings help germination and first growth of the crop, and help against weeds by enhancing the resilience of the crop.
5. Current regulations follow the “Not Unless” principle, which means not to use pesticides unless necessary.
6. We should pay attention to NVWA (Netherlands Food and Consumer Product Safety Authority) and CtgB (Board for the Authorisation of Plant Protection) during development to facilitate approvals before market entry.

## Prof. dr. Tom Ellis — Professor of Synthetic Genome Engineering working with bacterial cellulose at Imperial College London

## European Commission - DG SANTE - E4 Unit — Unit of Pesticides and Biocides at the European Union – Directorate: General for Health and Food Safety
Map group: Regulatory institutions

#### Why we reached out

We wanted to find out what regulations the bioinsecticide protein we are incorporating would be subject to, so that we can prepare our business timeline and market entry plan accordingly.

Main takeaways:

1. According to Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 of the European Parliament concerning the placing of plant protection products on the market, our protein would first need to get an approval as an active substance at the EU level. Then, it would need to get authorization of the pesticide at a Member State/zonal level.
2. First approval of an active substance takes between 2.5–3.5 years from the date of admissibility of application to publication. This approval can be granted for 10–15 years if the active substance is low risk. The approved active substance must be authorized as a “low-risk” plant protection product (or not) by the Member State.
3. Data requirements for active substances are stated in (the legal framework does not refer explicitly to proteins at the moment):

   1. Part A of the Annex to Regulation (EU) No 283/2013.
   2. Point 1.5 of the Introduction to the Annex has potential to reduce the amount of data required for proteins compared to conventional chemicals.
   3. More detail can be found at: https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/dyna2/pgd/

4. They are currently discussing an amendment to this Regulation that may facilitate further access to the market for biocontrol such as protein-based pesticides. It might still take a few years until the amendment becomes applicable even if it is adopted.

Reflection & integration: We have adjusted our timeline to include all steps that have to be taken to be compliant with the regulations.
