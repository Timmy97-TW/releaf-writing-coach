When we embarked on our project, we quickly realized the vast complexity surrounding the topic of natural rubber and the multitude of interconnected aspects that needed to be considered. Natural rubber production is not merely a technological challenge; it is intertwined with environmental, social, economic, and technological factors, each influencing the other in profound ways. To address these complexities effectively, we aimed to understand the implications of our work from as many perspectives as possible.

Affected Stakeholders
To achieve this, we conducted interviews and visits with 27 key stakeholders from diverse fields. These stakeholders included scientists, industry professionals, policymakers, farmers, non-governmental organizations, and community leaders. Their invaluable insights allowed us to gain a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and opportunities associated with natural rubber production and its potential alternatives.
Our human practices activities and considerations were thus divided into several interrelated areas:
- 1. Identifying the importance of rubber as a critical raw material: Understanding the indispensable role natural rubber plays in various industries and global supply chains.
- 2. Identifying the key challenges in engineering Dandelion: Exploring the biological, technological, and practical challenges in utilizing dandelion as a sustainable source of rubber.
- 3. Implementation in the real world: Examining the feasibility of translating our scientific innovations into practical solutions that can be adopted by the rubber industry.
- 4. The safety and security of Dandelion engineering: Ensuring that our project adheres to strict biosafety and biosecurity standards while addressing potential risks.
- 5. Political dimension: Considering how political decisions and international trade policies influence the global rubber market and the adoption of new alternatives.
- 6. Legal frameworks: Investigating the regulatory landscapes governing biotechnology, plant engineering, and natural rubber production.
- 7. Industry and economic stakeholders: Engaging with the business sector to understand the economic incentives, potential markets, and industrial adoption of dandelion-derived rubber.
- 8. The historical context of natural rubber production: Reflecting on the socio-economic history of rubber production, including its colonial past and long-standing global dependencies.
- 9. The social consequences and the affected stakeholders: Understanding the social impact of natural rubber production on farmers, workers, and communities, particularly in regions heavily reliant on its cultivation.

Each of these areas was deeply interconnected, with our explorations in one domain influencing our decisions and considerations in another. By thoroughly investigating these intertwined aspects, we gained a holistic understanding of the broader implications of our work.
This holistic approach allowed us to align our project with ethical, sustainable, and practical outcomes, ensuring that our solution addresses real-world challenges from multiple angles.
(1) Identifying the Importance of Rubber as a Critical Raw Material
At the outset of our project, we came across the 2023 Rubber as a Critical Raw Material Act biogenic strategic material (1), which underscored the importance of rubber as a vital resource. To better understand its implications, we consulted with the authors of this report.
These authors highlighted several key points:
- • The critical role of rubber in various industries.
- • The environmental problems associated with the cultivation of Hevea brasiliensis, including deforestation of rainforests and its negative impact on biodiversity.
- • The potential of alternative plants like dandelion for sustainable rubber production.
After exploring these issues, it became clear that we wanted to focus our efforts on addressing the current challenges in natural rubber production, with a particular emphasis on developing solutions through dandelion cultivation.
(2) Identifying the Key Challenges in Engineering Dandelion
To understand the key challenges of developing dandelion into a viable crop for rubber production, we began by exploring the practical hurdles of introducing a new crop like dandelion into real-world farming.
We spoke with Prof. Dirk Prüfer, an academic expert in dandelion biology and genetic engineering, who pointed out a significant gap: the lack of advanced genetic tools for dandelions. He also identified key engineering targets that could improve the plant's utility for rubber production, such as synchronizing flowering to allow for more efficient seed harvesting and increasing root size and volume to enhance rubber yield.

This conversation highlighted the importance of understanding the practical cultivation challenges. To move forward, we realized we needed to speak directly with those involved in dandelion breeding and cultivation to better understand the agricultural requirements for adapting this plant.
(3) Implementation in the Real World
To better understand the practical challenges of implementing dandelion as a viable crop for rubber production, we visited Dr. Fred Eickmeyer, a professional plant breeder and head of ESKUSA. He emphasized that breeding solely for rubber content has its limitations, as plants with high rubber levels often struggle to thrive in the field.
While our initial focus was on increasing rubber yield, these discussions brought to light other critical factors such as biomass production and the field variability of dandelion morphology. These are practical challenges that we hadn't anticipated in the lab. We learned that modifications to the leaf and root structures, whether through selective breeding or genetic engineering, would significantly improve the plant's suitability for mechanized harvesting, ultimately boosting yield and reducing labor costs.
Dr. Eickmeyer also pointed out another significant limitation in scaling up Taraxacum kok-saghyz (TKS) cultivation: the small seed size. This presents challenges for standard sowing practices, as the seeds require shallow planting, and conventional farming equipment struggles to handle them efficiently. This makes large-scale sowing difficult. Mechanizing both the sowing and harvesting processes will be crucial for the scalability of TKS cultivation.
After our discussion with Dr. Fred Eickmeyer, we sought to gain further insight from those directly working with dandelion. We reached out to farmers, including those already cultivating dandelion, to better understand the limitations they face in the field.
From these conversations, we learned that successful implementation requires input from multiple perspectives. Even a perfectly engineered plant can face challenges such as financial risks, the need for specialized equipment, and the complexities of root morphology. Farmers emphasized the complexity of introducing a new crop and stressed the importance of having comprehensive support and information throughout the process.
Additionally, dandelion farmers highlighted specific morphologies that limit large-scale harvesting, providing valuable insights into the practical barriers we would need to address.
Engaging with potential users early on made us realize the importance of involving stakeholders from the very beginning. Initially, we didn't fully understand the scope of these challenges, as they are often overlooked in scientific literature. It was only through consulting with experts and those directly involved in cultivation that we came to appreciate the full range of issues, from technical hurdles to real-world farming practices.
Listening to the general public
In addition to engaging with experts in agriculture and plant breeding, we wanted to understand the concerns of the general public regarding our project. Public acceptance is a crucial factor for the success of any scientific endeavor, especially one that involves genetic engineering and large-scale agricultural innovation.
To facilitate this, we organized an exhibition at the botanical garden, where we had the opportunity to interact with hundreds of people. This event allowed us to engage directly with the community and gather valuable feedback on their views, hopes, and concerns.
One recurring concern among attendees was biosafety, particularly the fear that dandelions could become invasive and spread uncontrollably if introduced into agriculture. The pappus, the seed dispersal mechanism of dandelions, was frequently cited as a key worry, as people were more concerned about the potential for dandelion spread compared to other plants.
This feedback reinforced the importance of addressing public concerns about environmental safety and emphasized the need to communicate how we plan to mitigate these risks. It also highlighted the role of transparent communication and ongoing dialogue with the public to build trust and ensure the societal acceptability of our project.
(4) Safety
After hearing public concerns about the potential invasiveness of dandelions, we sought to investigate whether dandelions truly pose a greater safety risk compared to other crops. To gain more insight into this issue, we consulted with expert Katja Thiele at the Julius Kühn Institute, who provided a deeper understanding of the potential risks involved.
We learned that TKS does not pose a greater risk than other crops. In fact, it is unable to outcompete local weeds, and cultivated crops in general are not well adapted to thrive in the wild. This makes it unlikely for TKS to grow outside a controlled agricultural environment, further underscoring its low risk in terms of spreading uncontrollably. Experts in this field concluded that the introduction of a novel Taraxacum species is highly unlikely to significantly impact the environment, as the German ecosystem is already well adapted to a wide variety of Taraxacum species.
In addition to biosafety concerns, other issues raised during our interviews and public engagement activities included questions about patents and ensuring that any engineered dandelion would be compatible with current EU legislation. We recognized the importance of addressing these regulatory and legal concerns to ensure both the safety and societal acceptability of our work.
(5+6) Political Dimension and Legal Framework
After receiving feedback highlighting the importance of considering the political and legal situation in Europe, we sought guidance from experts in the field of New Genomic Techniques (NGTs). Through several interviews, we contextualized our project within the evolving regulatory landscape, particularly regarding upcoming EU regulations on plants produced using NGTs.
We learned that the new NGT regulations would allow for the cultivation of plants engineered through cisgenesis. Cisgenesis refers to the genetic modification of a plant using genes from the same species or closely related species. This approach maintains the integrity of the plant's genetic makeup while enabling enhancements that could lead to improved agricultural performance.
In light of this information, we decided that our dandelion part collection would not incorporate synthetic regulatory elements. Instead, we aimed to characterize endogenous regulatory elements, which would still fall under the umbrella of cisgenesis and the regulations governing NGT1 plants. This choice aligns our project with the anticipated regulatory framework while supporting sustainable agricultural practices.
When discussing the patent aspects within the NGT framework, we learned that the new EU law aims to prevent patents on NGT-modified plants. This could facilitate easier development and commercialization of such plants, reducing the need for costly licensing agreements. To better understand how patents affect real-world implementation, we engaged with specialists in patent law, gaining insights into navigating the legal landscape for our project.
Finally, we sought to learn from the experiences of pioneers in synthetic biology, such as Dr. Ingo Potrykus and Dr. Richard Jefferson. Their insights illuminated how political and legal frameworks can impact the real-world applications of scientific projects and provided strategies for effectively addressing these challenges.
(7) Industry and Economic Stakeholders
Political and legal considerations significantly impact the rubber industry. One key regulation is the EU's Supply Chain Act, which mandates that companies demonstrate the origin and integrity of their entire supply chain for deforestation-free products. Additionally, the Human Rights Supply Chain Act, currently under development at the EU level, will require businesses to ensure human rights due diligence throughout their supply chains.
Another pressing issue is raw material resilience. Europe currently relies entirely on imported rubber, creating significant challenges for companies and driving the demand for alternative sources.
To understand how industrial stakeholders are affected by these geopolitical and legal factors, we engaged with representatives from Continental, a leading tire manufacturer. We discussed their motivation for investing in dandelion rubber, which stems from the unique properties of dandelion rubber and the pressing need to diversify rubber sources. In a conversation with Dr. Katharina Herzog, Project Manager at Continental, we explored the growing environmental pressures and political mandates requiring a reduction in the CO2 footprint of products.
Our discussions revealed that various sectors are actively seeking alternative rubber sources due to several factors, including climate change, diseases affecting the rubber tree, and political demands.
These conversations highlighted the critical need for innovation in rubber sourcing and the potential role of dandelion as a sustainable alternative to traditional rubber sources.
(8) Dandelion in Recent History
Natural rubber has been a vital raw material for industrial applications for an extended period. Recognizing this historical significance, we understood the need to explore the past of natural rubber and its supply chains. Historical supply chain disruptions have occurred before, prompting many countries that depend on imports to seek autonomy by investigating alternative rubber sources. This awareness drove us to delve into the history of dandelion rubber, a matter close to our hearts and consciousness.
During our visit to Prof. Dirk Prüfer, we discovered a crucial dimension of our project that we aimed to emphasize: the historical importance of dandelion rubber research. Prof. Prüfer introduced us to historical documents related to dandelion rubber research conducted by the National Socialists during World War II. This revelation awakened one of the most intriguing and multidimensional aspects of our project.
To deepen our understanding, we reached out to Dr. Susanne Heim, a leading expert on the Nazi regime's research into dandelion rubber. Inspired by Professor Prüfer's work, we sought Dr. Heim's insights into the ethical implications of utilizing knowledge gained under unethical conditions. She emphasized the necessity of historical awareness in scientific research and encouraged interdisciplinary collaboration to address complex moral issues.
This approach may not be typical for an iGEM team, but for us, it is essential to acknowledge the history surrounding our research topic.
While the past cannot be changed, we now have the opportunity to raise awareness and foster reflection on the ethical dimensions of our work.
(9) Social Consequences
The history of dandelion rubber is closely tied to its original area in Khastazan, where the removal of dandelion has had significant social implications. Through our interview with Kamila Magzieva from the Kazakh NGO KZ Rubber, we gained valuable insights into the responsibilities inherent in scientific research and the potential social consequences it can impose on specific populations. This experience underscored the importance of awareness and ethical considerations in our project.
As we move forward, we remain committed to continuously reflecting on these aspects. We ensured that our TKS seeds comply with the Nagoya Protocol, which emphasizes fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilization of genetic resources.
We also examined the current situation of Hevea brasiliensis rubber farmers, engaging with various NGOs to understand their challenges. Many farmers face a difficult situation, as their livelihoods depend on rubber tree farming, which is increasingly threatened by environmental changes and market pressures.
It is important for us to clarify that our project does not aim to replace rubber trees with dandelions. Instead, we seek to address the growing demand for rubber and the associated risks to its supply by developing an alternative source for this critical raw material. By creating a viable dandelion rubber industry, we hope to contribute to the resilience of rubber production while also promoting sustainable practices that benefit all stakeholders involved.
Integrated Human Practices
Our journey to develop dandelion as a viable rubber source has yielded several important insights through our engagement with various stakeholders. In our discussions with Prof. Dirk Prüfer, we uncovered a significant gap: the absence of advanced genetic tools specifically tailored for dandelions. This realization highlighted the critical need for targeted research and development in this area to facilitate progress.
Initially, our focus was on increasing rubber yield. However, conversations with Dr. Fred Eickmeyer, dandelion farmers, and farmers shifted our attention toward plant morphology and other essential agricultural traits. This prompted us to adjust our approach from merely boosting rubber yield to developing the tools necessary for engineering specific morphological traits in dandelions. Laying the foundation for targeted genetic engineering of dandelions is crucial for enhancing their overall viability as a crop.
After considering feedback from political and legal stakeholders, we opted not to use synthetic regulatory elements in our dandelion part collection. Instead, we chose to focus on characterizing endogenous regulatory elements, ensuring compliance with cisgenesis and NGT1 plant regulations. This strategic choice not only aligns our project with current regulatory frameworks but also facilitates the use in a real-world application context.
Moreover, we came to realize that developing a well-adapted dandelion plant is just one piece of a much larger puzzle. Successful implementation of our findings requires collaboration with a wide range of stakeholders, including farmers, industry representatives, and policymakers. Understanding the practical needs and concerns of those directly involved in dandelion cultivation is essential for fostering acceptance and ensuring the sustainability of our efforts. By engaging with these stakeholders early and often, we aim to create a more inclusive approach that addresses the diverse challenges associated with introducing dandelion as an alternative rubber source.
In summary, our exploration into dandelion rubber has reinforced the idea that successful agricultural innovation relies not just on scientific advancements but also on meaningful engagement with those who will ultimately implement and benefit from these innovations.
Outlook
Throughout our project, we conducted numerous interviews that provided valuable insights into the various dimensions of developing dandelion as a rubber source.
However, our efforts to engage with actual rubber farmers highlighted significant challenges. We reached out to several farmers but encountered barriers, particularly related to language differences and their concerns about potential negative consequences from participating in our research. This has underscored the importance of addressing these barriers to foster more inclusive dialogue and gather a broader range of perspectives.
Moving forward, we recognize the critical role that political decision-makers play in shaping agricultural practices and policies. We aim to approach these stakeholders more actively to understand the reasons behind their decisions and engage them in constructive dialogues about biotechnology. As highlighted by Dr. Ingo Potrykus, co-developer of Golden Rice, it is crucial to ensure informed decisions that benefit both society and the environment. If given the opportunity, we would organize workshops or panel discussions to share our findings and engage relevant stakeholders in conversations about the potential of dandelion as a sustainable source of rubber.
Moreover, we are excited about the potential to explore additional uses for dandelions beyond rubber extraction. The opportunity to utilize the whole plant could open new avenues for sustainability and economic viability. Given more time, we would delve into researching dandelion's other applications, such as its medicinal properties or potential as a biofuel source. Engaging with researchers and businesses in these fields could provide a holistic view of dandelion's potential and create additional value streams.
In summary, while our project has aimed to lay the groundwork for the implementation of dandelion as a sustainable rubber source, we recognize that there is much more to explore and develop.
By expanding our outreach to various stakeholders and considering the broader implications of our work, we could create an even further outreaching framework that aligns with the needs of all involved parties.
Our goal is to ensure that dandelion cultivation not only addresses the demand for rubber but also supports sustainable agricultural practices and contributes positively to the communities involved.
