One of the core ideals of iGEM, and what sets it apart from other purely scientific initiatives, is its focus on the impact that synthetic biology has on society. While students are encouraged to think big and tackle global problems, they are also expected to carefully consider the ramifications of their work. By gathering feedback from stakeholders, the teams can guide their project to properly address tangible issues. This helps them maintain focus in developing solutions that are actually relevant. The framework we use to ground our project to real challenges is laid out by the UN as 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The sustainable development goals were first implemented in 2015 with the creation of the UN 2030 Agenda. Their aim is to guide a reduction of poverty and inequality though shared economic prosperity by the year of 2030, while also limiting the impact on the environment.
We strongly believe that our project contributes to fulfilling three of these goals. Because natural rubber is a valuable resource and its production is closely tied to both environmental and societal issues, the idea of introducing dandelions as an alternative crop has the potential to advance these SDGs.
8 – Decent Work and Economic Growth
The 8th SDG covers the creation of a solid economic foundation upon which the remaining SDGs can be achieved by countries. It highlights the need for building a healthy economy in developing countries, so that they have the resources for solving social issues. This strategy involves incentives in economic diversification, technological upgrading, entrepreneurship, and improvements in work conditions.
Rubber is an essential raw material to all sectors of industry. Despite the key role synthetic rubber plays in it, the superior malleability and heat-resistance of natural rubber makes it indispensable to a plethora of applications, from the medical field to the aerospace industry. Due to the geographical limitation for growing rubber trees, the majority of the global supply is controlled by few countries, making the access to this resource a point of geopolitical contention. In 2015, the European Commission included Natural rubber in the list of "Critical Raw Resources" due to the EU's total reliance on imports to supply its industry with high quality latex. Therefore, we believe that the introduction of an alternative to Hevea brasiliensis, such as dandelion, would not only alleviate the dependance on imports, but also boost local economies with rubber production.
While mechanization made its processing more efficient over the years, tapping the trees to extract the raw latex is still done trough manual labor, employing 30 million people worldwide ("Natural Rubber Supply Chain," n.d.). This practice has been tied to numerous cases of human rights violations, such as poor work conditions, improper use of safety equipment leading to exposure to toxic chemicals, and child labor (1, 2). A number of these problems are tied to the grueling manual labor necessary to tap dozens of trees and extract the latex. In that regard, dandelion allows for less strenuous working conditions, as plucking the whole plant from the soil is a simpler method of harvesting. This opens up the possibility of using existing mechanized harvesting equipment, which will reduce the need for human input. Nonetheless, we must keep in mind when proposing such solutions that a shift to mechanization may threaten the livelihood of current producers by introducing competition. This was the main concerned raised by Stefan Hörmann from the NGO Fair Rubber, which works towards improving the work conditions of workers in rubber tree plantations in South-East Asia.
With increased industrialization, the demand for natural rubber as a raw material will starkly increase. We believe that, rather than supplanting the current Hevea brasiliensis plantations, dandelion rubber crops could serve to absorb the growing demand for this resource without hurting the present producing regions. In fact, the global natural rubber market is projected to grow by 53% in the next eight years, reaching 25.2 billion USD by 2032 (3). Moreover, higher GDP per capita will come in tandem with increased car ownership. Considering that today, 70% of natural rubber is used by the tire industry, the expansion of natural rubber production will be necessary t0 support the projected economic growth. The use of dandelion addresses these points directly, as it enables the growth of this industry to new regions where the cultivation of rubber trees is not feasible. This allows countries that previously could not support domestic production to supply their industry and diminish their reliance on importation.
12 – Responsible Consumption and Production
As highlighted above, preserving the environment while promoting economic and industrial development is one of the pillars of the SDGs. Goal 12 pertains to ensuring that we achieve "sustainable management and efficient use of natural resources". In that context, we are encouraged to consider the waste produced by economic activities, and work towards reducing it.
Along with the above-mentioned expansion of the rubber industry, thousands of tons of extra waste will be added to the already serious issue of waste disposal. Annually, 9.2 million tons of rubber waste are created worldwide, out of which only 1.5% are recycled (4). In the EU, tens of millions of Euros have been invested in measures to recycle rubber products, especially used vehicle tires. However, finding suitable applications for recycled materials remains a challenge. For instance, while the rubber granules from repurposed tires were widely used to fill in turfgrass in soccer pitches, recent studies have demonstrated that carcinogenic compounds in the tire can result in health risks for athletes (5, 6).
It is important to recognize that the use of alternative sources of natural rubber does not solve this pressing issue, and we believe that measures, such as the initiatives from the EU, are central to making the lifecycle of rubber products more sustainable. Additionally, we believe that synthetic biology can play a role in tackling this challenge as well, as highlighted by previous iGEM teams (Keystone 2021 and Brasil-USP 2015), which developed innovative methods for rubber degradation.
Nonetheless, we believe that the shift of natural rubber production from Hevea brasiliensis trees to dandelion could greatly improve the sustainability of the rubber industry. The process of transforming the raw plant sap into processed latex is fundamentally different in dandelion when compared to rubber trees. The traditional method uses high quantities of chemicals and water, making the treatment of this wastewater expensive. Instances where this step was neglected have resulted in the release of toxic waste directly to waterways, leading to damage to the environment and to the health of the local population. Meanwhile, dandelion rubber can be extracted via hot-water extraction, which is much less polluting (7). With the expansion of this method of production, expanding the use of dandelion as a rubber crop could help achieve the 12th SDG.
13 & 15 Climate Action & Life On Land
Finally, the growing global demand for rubber is a major driver of deforestation in tropical regions, with serious consequences on biodiversity, carbon capture and soil erosion. In the past 30 years, it has resulted in the clearing of vast swaths of rainforest in southern Asia for expanding rubber tree plantations. It is estimated that the deforested area amounts to 40 thousand square kilometers, roughly the size of Switzerland.
Because of the resilience of dandelion to broad climate conditions, we believe that the expansion in natural rubber production could be shifted to new areas, preventing the clearing out of forests in key biodiversity areas. Moreover, due to its ability to grow despite poor soil conditions, dandelion crops could be grown in previously unused areas, so as not to compete for land with food production.
Despite its significant impact on the environment, natural rubber only represents 48% of the total rubber consumption worldwide, with the other 52% being synthetic rubber. Unfortunately, the petroleum used for its manufacture is a major contributor to climate change, accounting for more than 700 million barrels of oil every year. Investing in more sustainable alternatives, such as dandelion, could reduce the reliance on synthetic rubber, as the supply of natural latex could be drastically increased.
