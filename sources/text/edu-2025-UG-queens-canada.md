# Education
Everyone Learns Differently, Here's How We Reach Them
Synthetic biology is only as powerful as it is understood. The field holds tremendous potential to address global challenges, but like any emerging technology, it raises important questions about safety, ethics, and societal impact. For synthetic biology to develop responsibly, it can't just be shaped by scientists in labs, it needs input from everyone it will affect. This realization drove our education initiatives. We set out to bridge the gap between research and community, bringing diverse voices into conversations about synthetic biology's future. From elementary students to the general public, we designed programs for all levels of understanding and backgrounds, ensuring that synthetic biology education isn't limited to those already in the field.
We recognized that people learn differently, so we tailored our outreach accordingly. We provided hands-on labs for kinesthetic learners, video lectures for auditory learners, and illustrated tutorials for visual learners. By partnering with community organizations, we built educational infrastructure designed to outlast our team and continue bringing diverse voices into synthetic biology.
With our efforts we managed to reach over 22,000 people across all social platforms, videos, and workshops. We hope to ensure that everyone can understand and help shape where this technology goes.

## Kinesthetic Learning
### Lets Talk Science Kits
#### Workshop 1:
Description
Our Designing Life workshop was developed for Let's Talk Science. It introduces elementary students to synthetic biology fundamentals via an interactive “BioBrick” activity. Students learn how simple genetic parts can be combined to build biological systems through an age-appropriate hands-on puzzle exercises. The workshop covers the basics DNA while allowing for easy to follow steps to combine different genetic parts, or in this case puzzle pieces. The workshop concept was inspired by the 2022 UBCO iGEM team Let's Talk Science workshop. We expanded the gene bank and integrated an AI image generator component allowing the kids to see their designs come to life.
[IMG] LTS Workshop 1
Knowledge Gap
Young students rarely see how engineering and biology connect, and synthetic biology is often perceived as inaccessible or “too advanced.” This workshop addresses that gap by translating core concepts into concrete, playful tasks that demystify the field. By starting early, we aim to demonstrate that broaden the pipeline of future learners who feel comfortable contributing to synthetic biology.
Learning & Dialogue
Students practiced explaining their designs aloud, asked questions, and iterated on ideas, creating genuine two-way dialogue between learners and facilitators. Teachers reported increased curiosity and clearer student language around biological concepts after the activity. The hands-on format encouraged peer discussion and made abstract ideas tangible.
Future Directions
All lesson plans, slide decks, and activity templates have been documented and donated to Let's Talk Science and our wiki so future teams can reuse or adapt them. Teams can run the same workshop, translate materials to other languages, or extend the activity into multi-day modules. Donated kits ensure quick setup and sustainable outreach use across classrooms.

#### Workshop 2:
Description
Antibody Design guides kids (≈8-12) through what antibodies and antigens are using analogies, tactile materials, and a “design your own antigen/antibody” activity where students build shapes and matching Y-shaped antibodies. The workshop combines a short interactive lecture, an “Immune Tag” movement game, and a gallery walk where students explain their designs and intended function. Detailed plans and slide decks document the workflow for classroom facilitators.
Knowledge Gap
Elementary students often hold simplistic or media-driven views of immunity and may not understand specificity or how antibodies work in real applications. This workshop fills that gap by making molecular recognition tangible and connecting design thinking to real biomedical uses like treatments and diagnostics.
[IMG] LTS Workshop 2
Learning & Dialogue
Through building and explaining their antigen-antibody pairs, students practice scientific reasoning, peer teaching, and communicating function (block, neutralize, tag), which fosters mutual learning between facilitators and learners. The gallery walk and wrap-up discussion prompt questions about real-world applications and ethical uses of biotechnology.
Future Directions
All materials (presenter slides, activity plans, and supply lists) are documented for upload to the wiki and for donation to Let's Talk Science; future teams can scale the activity, incorporate simple data collection, or link the workshop to junior wet-lab demos or sequencing outreach.
#### Final Workshop:
Description
Glow Germs uses GloGerm lotion and a UV blacklight to visualize how invisible “germs” spread via touch and how proper handwashing reduces transmission; the session pairs a short talk with an interactive demonstration and hand-washing practice. Presenters contextualize the activity for public health by showing how pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa can affect vulnerable populations such as people with cystic fibrosis. The workshop follows a clear stepwise protocol for classroom delivery.
Knowledge Gap
Children often underestimate how easily microbes spread and may hold misconceptions about when and how to wash hands effectively. This activity fills that gap with a visible, memorable demonstration that connects everyday hygiene to community health and vulnerability.
Learning & Dialogue
Students see the invisible transmission of “germs,” test hypotheses about spread, and practice improved washing technique, prompting questions about infection prevention and empathy for at-risk groups. The interactive format generates lively discussion, encourages caregivers to reinforce practices, and links simple behaviour to broader health outcomes.
Future Directions
The workshop protocol, presenter script, and library workflow are documented for easy reuse; donated kits and stepwise materials make classroom setup straightforward and scalable. Future teams can adapt timing and emphasis (e.g., focus on respiratory vs. surface transmission), produce companion videos, or integrate evaluation surveys to measure behaviour change over time.
### QSEA
Description
We introduced QGEM and this year's project to the Queen's Summer Engineering Academy (QSEA) senior group, providing context on synthetic biology and biomedical engineering to these high school students. We focused on delayed-release pharmaceuticals, which we demonstrated through an interactive activity where students designed their own “pills” using everyday materials as enteric coatings. Each design was tasked with releasing ingredients at three distinct time intervals (30s, 90s, 180s), simulating real-world drug delivery mechanisms and encouraging creative problem-solving within their subteams.
Knowledge Gap
Many students are familiar with traditional engineering fields but have limited exposure to pharmaceutical and therapeutic applications of synthetic biology. This workshop addressed this gap by introducing the science behind drug release, design considerations, and its impact on treating diseases present in various parts of the human body. Our goal was to spark interest in this less-promoted but impactful branch of engineering while showing its real-world relevance.
Learning & Dialogue
Students gained hands-on experience in how delayed-release drugs function, deepening their understanding of drug design, timing, and targeting within the body. The activity encouraged curiosity, critical thinking, and problem-solving, while also fostering discussion on the practical applications of therapeutics and biomedical engineering. We hoped students would leave inspired to explore this field further and apply these concepts in future studies or research.
Future Directions
This event can be easily replicated with inexpensive, widely available materials, making it accessible for a variety of programs. Future teams could introduce additional variables, such as pH or enzyme simulations, to more accurately mimic physiological conditions and explore advanced drug-release strategies. Expanding partnerships with other STEM programs or incorporating connections to current research could broaden impact and provide students with insights into real-world biomedical engineering applications.
### WiSE Workshop
Description
QGEM partnered with Queen's WiSE (Women in STEM Engineering) for their annual Badge Day, a day-long event for Girl Guides in Kingston ages 5 to 9 filled with STEM experiments, interactive games, and inspiring guest speakers. Our team worked with 50 students in a tower-building workshop, where participants were tasked with designing the tallest free-standing tower using limited construction materials. Unlike other sessions in which university mentors guided participants throughout, our QGEM team introduced a more independent structure by rotating mentors, encouraging the children to problem-solve and collaborate without constant direction. This workshop introduced the engineering design process: defining the problem, researching solutions, designing, prototyping, testing, and iterating. It fostered creativity, resilience, and adaptability. To further challenge participants, we introduced new restrictions mid-activity, requiring them to redesign and adapt their structures under new constraints, reinforcing the importance of perseverance and critical thinking.
Knowledge Gap
Women continue to be underrepresented in engineering due to factors such as inadequate early exposure to STEM concepts and persistent stereotypes surrounding technical careers. By introducing fundamental engineering principles to girls at an early age, this workshop aimed to close that gap and highlight the accessibility of problem-solving in STEM. As a synthetic biology design team, QGEM is committed to creating tangible interventions that normalize women's presence in technical spaces. A survey of our own team revealed that 61% of our members identify as women, one of the highest proportions among Queen's engineering design teams, demonstrating that meaningful change is possible. Concepts such as iterative problem-solving, adaptability under constraints, and independent reasoning are rarely taught at such an early age. This workshop offered participants the opportunity to build these skills in a playful, hands-on environment that framed engineering as both challenging and enjoyable.
Learning & Dialogue
The tower-building competition created an open environment where participants actively engaged in questioning, testing, and refining their ideas. Mentors guided reflection by asking participants to explain why they chose specific designs and how they might improve them. This dialogue encouraged children to think critically about design trade-offs, balance creativity with practicality, and recognize failure as an integral part of the learning process. Many participants expressed excitement when their towers collapsed, viewing it not as defeat but as an opportunity to try again with a new approach. This reframing of “failure” into an experimental step supported growth mindset development and promoted confidence in tackling unfamiliar challenges. The activity also sparked conversations about real-world applications of engineering, such as how bridges, skyscrapers, and other structures rely on the same principles, which helped participants connect their hands-on experience to the world around them. In addition, we introduced participants to what iGEM is and how our QGEM design team approaches projects. Using pictures and simplified workflows, we highlighted how our own design process mirrors theirs; iterating, testing, and continuously improving our science, emphasizing that curiosity and persistence are central to both engineering and biology.
[IMG] Wise Workshop
Future Directions
Building on the success of this year's event, QGEM plans to continue its partnership with Queen's WiSE while incorporating elements of synthetic biology into future challenges. While we initially avoided highly technical content out of concern it might alienate younger participants, our experiences with other outreach initiatives such as MiniQ and the Science Fair showed that children are highly receptive to learning seemingly complex ideas when presented in interactive, age-appropriate ways. Future workshops may integrate synthetic biology concepts through simplified models—for example, simulating genetic circuits with colored blocks or using storytelling to introduce biological “design challenges.” We are also developing workshops with other Queen's clubs that focus on underrepresented groups in STEM, further expanding access to early experiences with synthetic biology and engineering. By continuing to refine our programming and collaborate across campus, we aim to create a pipeline of engaging, accessible activities that inspire the next generation of diverse scientists and engineers.

### DIY Lab Handbook
Description
QGEM compiled and refined a series of low-cost DIY laboratory kits to make hands-on science accessible outside of formal lab settings. These kits allow students to perform real experiments and explore foundational techniques from the comfort of their own homes while engaging in kinesthetic learning. Our first prototype was a hand-powered centrifuge assembled from basic materials, paired with a step-by-step handbook to guide construction and use. We then optimized the protocols for a low cost gel electrophoresis experiment and incubator.
[IMG] DIY Lab Kit
Knowledge Gap
Access to laboratory-grade equipment is a major barrier for students interested in pursuing STEM outside of school or university labs. Standard equipment such as centrifuges, gel rigs, and incubators are cost-prohibitive, fragile, and difficult to source. Young learners often have limited opportunities to interact with these tools before higher education, limiting their ability to connect classroom knowledge with real-world experimentation. By creating functional DIY versions at a fraction of the cost, QGEM addressed this gap, showing that key scientific techniques can be taught using affordable materials. These interventions not only expand access to hands-on science but also demystify laboratory technologies, showing students that science can be approachable and adaptable.
Learning & Dialogue
These kits serve as an entry point for more people to participate in real scientific inquiry, regardless of access to formal lab spaces. By compiling open-source protocols and demonstrating how complex equipment can be replicated at low cost, we aim to make the tools of science more transparent and attainable. This approach challenges the perception that meaningful experimentation is limited to professionals or well-funded labs, instead showing that science is a process anyone can engage with.
Future Directions
This work is still in its early stages, but it lays the groundwork for something much bigger. Going forward, we hope to improve the quality and usability of our existing kits, add more tools, and collaborate with other iGEM teams to create a shared database of low-cost, open-source lab equipment. Our goal is to build a community around making science more accessible, so that anyone, anywhere, can explore real experiments and be part of the scientific process without needing access to expensive infrastructure.
### Lab Workshop
Description
The Discovery Lab Workshop provides high school students and community members with hands-on exposure to fundamental laboratory techniques through a structured 2.5-hour experience. Participants rotate through four stations: precision pipetting with data analysis, pH testing using litmus paper, anatomical dissection (eye specimens), and microscopy observation including gel electrophoresis results. Each 20-25 minute station combines demonstration with guided practice, ensuring students gain practical experience with essential lab skills while working in small groups with dedicated instructors.
Knowledge Gap
Many students lack access to authentic laboratory experiences that could inspire STEM career pathways. Traditional classroom settings often cannot provide the specialized equipment, materials, and expert instruction needed for meaningful hands-on learning. This workshop addresses that barrier by bringing students into a state of the art university laboratory environment where they can experience the precision, methodology, and excitement of scientific research firsthand.
Learning & Dialogue
Students develop technical competencies in pipetting accuracy, pH analysis, dissection techniques, and microscopic observation while building scientific vocabulary and analytical thinking skills. The rotation format encourages peer discussion and instructor interaction, with activities designed to prompt hypothesis formation (such as comparing mystery substance behavior to known liquids) and anatomical structure identification. Safety protocols and proper lab practices are integrated throughout, giving students professional-level training in laboratory conduct and equipment handling.
Future Directions
This workshop model can be scaled by developing partnerships with additional academic departments and creating standardized training materials for team leads. Future iterations could incorporate pre- and post-assessments to measure learning outcomes, expand to include additional techniques like basic DNA extraction or microscope slide preparation, and develop take-home materials that reinforce concepts learned. Establishing relationships with multiple high schools could create a sustainable pipeline for regular workshops, while documenting best practices and safety protocols would enable other institutions to replicate this hands-on approach to STEM outreach.
## Auditory Learning
### Genes to Genius
Description
Genes to Genius is a video project designed to make synthetic biology accessible by explaining it at five levels of complexity: child, teenager, undergraduate, graduate, and researcher. Inspired by WIRED's “Explains in 5 Levels” series, our goal was to brake down barriers of jargon and make synthetic biology approachable for a broad audience.
Knowledge Gap
Many people view synthetic biology as intimidating or “unnatural,” often because of the word “synthetic” itself. We noticed that there was a lack of trust in the synthetic biology field, partly because there are very few conversational explanations of the field that people could listen to and understand at their own level. By addressing this gap, we aimed to help people engage in discussions about the fields strengths and its safety concerns.
Learning & Dialogue
The video sparked curiosity and genuine dialogue generating close to 6,000 social media impressions. By progressing through increasing levels of complexity, we created space for audiences to ask questions and challenge their own assumptions. This structure promoted mutual learning and showed how synthetic biology can be accessible at all levels.
Future Directions
Future iGEM teams can replicate this model by producing their own “explains at 5 levels” videos on a variety of synthetic biology topics, tailoring the auditory explanations to local audiences or languages. Building on the proven success of the WIRED format, this approach can effectively explain all aspects of iGEM, from dry lab and human practices to wet lab work. These videos can then be shared as classroom resources, outreach tools, or podcasts, making synthetic biology more accessible to diverse audiences.

## Reading/Writing Learning
### APSC 103
Description
In Winter 2026, our team will serve as industry clients for APSC 103, a first-year engineering design course at Queen's University. We proposed a real-world challenge inspired by iGEM's engineering design cycle: build a functional, affordable spectrophotometer with a $100 CAD budget. While the hands-on prototyping is central, an equally important part of the course is communication: students are required to document their design process, reflect on their decision-making, and present their work in written reports.
Knowledge Gap
Many first-year engineering students have limited opportunities to explore disciplines outside of their coursework. Over the past year, we noticed a lack of interest in synthetic biology from engineering students, which was supported by informal feedback from students who expressed concern about the biology-heavy nature of the field. However, synthetic biology thrives on interdisciplinary skills, including engineering, design, and applied problem-solving which engineering students are especially well suited for.
Learning & Dialogue
As clients, we will mentor students during biweekly meetings and provide feedback during midterm and final presentations. By working with us as clients rather than instructors, they experience the collaborative, real-world dynamic of engineering in research and industry. This dialogue showcases how engineering design principles directly contribute to synthetic biology and encourages students to see themselves as active contributors to scientific discovery.
Future Directions
Future iGEM teams can replicate this model by serving as clients proposing new synthetic biology-inspired design challenges for engineering classes to increase exposure and engagement. Looking even further down the line, these written outputs could form a growing database of low-cost lab tools, permanently integrating synthetic biology into engineering education while also providing future students with written resources that guide both technical design and scientific communication.

### Pipetting Video Tutorial
Description
The Pipetting Tutorial Video demonstrated essential micropipetting techniques through a structured educational recording. Our wet lab and outreach team created a video covering three critical pipetting methods: the blow-out technique for standard applications, first-stop-only dispensing for volatile solutions, and priming procedures for enhanced accuracy. The tutorial goes through the components of a pipette, volume setting, common error prevention, and proper tip disposal, providing a reusable training resource for future students.
Knowledge Gap
Many students encounter micropipettes for the first time without understanding the precision and technique required for accurate liquid handling. Poor pipetting technique can compromise experimental results and create safety hazards, yet detailed instruction is often unavailable outside formal laboratory courses, and due to the interdisciplinary nature of iGEM, many of our members are not exposed to this formal training. This video resource provides an accessible, repeatable training resource that students can reference before, during, and after hands-on sessions to reinforce proper methodology and build confidence with this fundamental laboratory skill.
Learning & Dialogue
The video production process required team members to articulate complex technical procedures clearly and demonstrate each step with precision. Creating visual content forced instructors to break down techniques into discrete, teachable components while considering diverse experience levels. The recording serves as both a teaching tool and a reference to review proper technique.
Future Directions
This video format can be expanded to cover additional laboratory techniques featured in the workshop, creating a comprehensive digital library of instructional content. Future productions could incorporate close-up camera angles, slow-motion demonstration of critical steps, and common mistake examples to enhance learning effectiveness. The video library could be shared with partner schools for pre-workshop preparation or post-workshop review, while creating standardized content that enables program replication at other institutions and maintains instructional quality across different facilitators.
